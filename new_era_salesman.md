## A Guide to Selling Bojangles to Everyone, One Business Card at a Time

**Prepared for Bojangles Corporate Sales Teams, Franchise Operators, and Marketing Leadership**

# PREFACE: SELLING THE SOUTH, ONE RELATIONSHIP AT A TIME

Bojangles does not need to convince anyone that a scratch-made biscuit tastes better than a frozen one. The product sells itself the moment a customer bites into a Cajun Filet Biscuit that was hand-breaded that morning and served on a biscuit baked twenty minutes ago. What the brand needs is a **systematic way to bring that moment to more people**—to the office manager who is tired of ordering the same sandwich platters, to the family that wants a gallon of sweet tea with dinner, to the neighborhood that has never tasted a Bo-Berry Biscuit but would love it if someone brought one to their door.

This book is a guide to that work. It is not a theoretical treatise on sales methodology. It is a practical manual for the person who picks up the phone, walks into the office, or stands behind the counter with a business card and a sample. The book is organized around a single conviction: **sales at Bojangles happens one call at a time, one business card proposal at a time, one software feature at a time.** There is no shortcut. There is no mass campaign that replaces the personal relationship. The corporate account that orders breakfast for fifty people every month started with one person who walked into an office with a dozen biscuits and a smile.

The book covers five disciplines. **Part One** establishes the sales foundation: how to find customers, how to open a conversation, and how to close a sale without pressure. **Part Two** introduces the Southern Love Delivery System: a model for delivering large-format drinks and family meals to surrounding addresses, including the half-gallon drink box that makes it possible. **Part Three** details the sales attributes and practices that separate a professional from an order-taker. **Part Four** surveys the modern sales mediums and teaches how to develop marketing materials that work. **Part Five** concludes with the philosophy that ties it all together: selling Bojangles is not selling food. It is selling the feeling of being taken care of, Southern style.

The Main Ingredient Test applies to every sales call and every marketing piece: does this make the customer feel cared for, or merely sold to? If it does, it belongs. If it does not, it is noise.

# PART ONE: THE SALES FOUNDATION

## Chapter 1: Finding the Customers Who Are Already Looking for You

The most common mistake in restaurant sales is assuming that customers must be convinced to buy. In reality, the customers who need Bojangles are already looking for what Bojangles offers. They are the office manager who needs breakfast for a 7:30 a.m. meeting. They are the construction foreman who needs fifty boxed lunches delivered to a job site. They are the parent who needs a fundraiser partner for the school's spring carnival. They are the family that wants a Sunday dinner that tastes like someone's grandmother made it, without anyone having to spend the afternoon in the kitchen.

The salesperson's job is not to create the need. The job is to **be present when the need arises**. That presence is built through systematic prospecting within the store's trade area. The store should maintain a prospect list of **fifty to one hundred organizations** within a three-mile radius, organized by category: corporate offices, medical practices, schools, churches, construction sites, and community organizations. The list should include the name of the contact person, the best time to reach them, and the date of the last contact. The list is not a database to be admired. It is a **call sheet to be worked**.

Corporate accounts are the highest-value target because they order consistently and in volume. A single office that orders breakfast for its monthly all-hands meeting generates **$150 to $400 per order** and can be counted on to reorder if the first experience is flawless. The buyer is not the CEO. The buyer is the **office manager, the executive assistant, or the HR coordinator** who is responsible for making the meeting happen without a hitch. These buyers are reachable through LinkedIn, through the building directory, or through a simple walk-in with a menu and a sample . The goal of the first contact is not a sale. It is **permission to follow up**.

## Chapter 2: The First Conversation—How to Open Without Pitching

The first conversation with a potential customer should not sound like a sales pitch. It should sound like a neighbor introducing themselves. The most effective opening is disarmingly simple: "Hi, I'm [name] from the Bojangles on [street]. I brought you a dozen biscuits because I wanted you to know we're here and we cater for offices. If you ever need breakfast for a meeting, I'm the person to call." The sample costs the store approximately eight dollars in food cost. The potential lifetime value of the account is thousands. The sample is not a promotion. It is an **investment in a relationship**.

The second conversation should happen within one week. The message is not "Did you like the biscuits?" It is "I wanted to make sure you got the sample and to give you my direct number. If you have a meeting coming up, I can have breakfast there by 7:30 with one call." The goal is to move from a transactional interaction to a **personal connection**. The office manager who has the store manager's cell phone number is far more likely to order than the office manager who has to navigate a website. The direct number is the difference between being a vendor and being a partner.

The first order should be treated as a **proving ground**. The store must execute flawlessly: on-time delivery, correct items, hot food, clean packaging, and a follow-up call after the delivery to confirm satisfaction. A single late delivery or missing item on a first catering order can lose the account permanently . The store manager should personally oversee the first order, and the delivery should include a **thank-you card with a direct reorder number**. The goal is not just to fulfill the order but to demonstrate that Bojangles catering is reliable in a way that the competitor's catering is not.

## Chapter 3: The One-Call-at-a-Time Discipline

The phrase "one call at a time" is not a slogan. It is an operational discipline. The salesperson who tries to close every prospect on the first call will fail, because trust is built through repetition, not pressure. The salesperson who makes one call, sends one sample, and follows up one time will build a pipeline that compounds over months and years. The discipline is simple: **every day, make ten contacts**. Ten calls, ten walk-ins, ten emails. The contacts do not need to be perfect. They need to be consistent. A store that makes ten contacts per day will make 250 contacts per month. If even five percent convert to first orders, that is twelve new accounts per month. If half of those become recurring, that is six new recurring accounts per month, which is **$18,000 to $48,000 in annualized revenue** from one month of disciplined prospecting.

The one-call-at-a-time discipline also applies to the existing customer base. The salesperson who calls a current account two weeks before their typical order date and says, "I know your team meeting is coming up. Would you like the same breakfast package as last time?" is practicing the discipline of **proactive service**. The customer does not have to remember to order. The store has remembered for them. That is the difference between a vendor and a partner. The proactive call is worth more than any advertising campaign because it is personal, timely, and effortless for the customer.

The discipline requires **tracking**. Every call, every sample, every follow-up should be logged in a simple system—a spreadsheet, a CRM, or a notebook. The log should record the date, the contact, the outcome, and the next action. The log is not bureaucracy. It is the **memory of the sales effort**. Without it, the salesperson repeats the same calls, forgets the follow-ups, and loses the relationships that took months to build. With it, the pipeline becomes visible, and the salesperson can see where the effort is paying off and where it needs to be redirected.

## Chapter 4: Closing the Sale Without Pressure

The close in restaurant sales is not a dramatic moment of persuasion. It is a **natural next step** that the customer takes because the salesperson has made it easy. The most effective close is a question that assumes the answer is yes: "Would you like me to put together a sample order for your next team meeting? I can have it there by 7:30." The customer who says yes has not been pressured. They have been **invited**. The invitation is the close.

The close should always include a **specific next step**. "Let me know if you're interested" is not a close. "I'll call you on Tuesday to confirm the headcount" is a close. The specific next step moves the relationship forward and gives the salesperson a reason to follow up. The customer who agrees to a specific next step is a customer who is **committing without feeling committed**. The commitment is small, but it is the foundation for the larger orders that will follow.

The close should also include a **reorder mechanism**. The customer who orders once should know exactly how to order again. The reorder mechanism can be a direct phone number, a text message, or a saved order in the app. The simpler the mechanism, the more likely the customer will use it. The goal is to remove every friction point between the customer's need and the store's fulfillment. A customer who has to search for the phone number is a customer who might order from the competitor instead.

The final element of the close is **follow-through**. The salesperson who promises a sample by Tuesday and delivers it on Monday is a salesperson who will be trusted with the larger order. The salesperson who promises and forgets is a salesperson who will not be called again. Follow-through is not a sales technique. It is a **character trait**. It is the trait that separates the salesperson who builds a book of business from the salesperson who spends their career chasing one-time orders.

# PART TWO: THE SOUTHERN LOVE DELIVERY SYSTEM

## Chapter 5: Why Delivery Is the Next Frontier for Bojangles

Bojangles already offers delivery through third-party partners including DoorDash, Uber Eats, Postmates, Grubhub, and Google Ordering . These partnerships provide reach, but they also introduce variability: the delivery driver may be late, the food may sit in a hot bag too long, and the customer experience is shaped by a stranger rather than by Bojangles. The Southern Love Delivery System is a **first-party delivery capability** that gives the brand control over the entire experience, from the kitchen to the customer's door.

The system is designed for a specific type of order: **large-format, family-sized, and drink-inclusive**. A customer who orders two chicken sandwiches and two drinks is a customer for third-party delivery. A customer who orders a family meal with a half-gallon of sweet tea, a half-gallon of lemonade, and a dozen biscuits is a customer for the Southern Love Delivery System. The difference is volume, margin, and the opportunity to deliver a **complete Southern meal experience** rather than a collection of individual items.

The target range for the Southern Love Delivery System is a **three-to-five-mile radius** around each store, with delivery available during defined hours (for example, 11 a.m. to 8 p.m. for lunch and dinner, and 7 a.m. to 10 a.m. for breakfast catering). The radius is not arbitrary. It is based on the time it takes to deliver a hot meal without compromising quality. A delivery that takes thirty minutes is acceptable. A delivery that takes forty-five minutes is not, because the fried chicken will have lost its crispness and the biscuits will have cooled. The radius should be defined by **drive time, not distance**, and it should be reviewed quarterly as traffic patterns change.

## Chapter 6: The Half-Gallon Drink Box—Engineering a Delivery-Ready Beverage

The centerpiece of the Southern Love Delivery System is the **half-gallon drink box**: a foldable, liquid-tight container with a filling cap lid that locks the first fill, branded with the Bojangles identity, and designed for the family-sized beverage orders that are the heart of Southern dining. The box addresses a gap in the current delivery offering. A family that orders a meal for four people wants a beverage that serves four people, not four individual cups that will spill, sweat, and arrive at different temperatures. The half-gallon box delivers the volume, the brand, and the convenience that the family occasion requires.

The engineering of the box is straightforward but must be precise. The box is constructed from a **food-grade paperboard** with a **plastic coating on the interior** that provides a liquid-tight barrier. The box is delivered flat and folded for storage, then unfolded and filled at the store. The filling cap is a **one-way valve** that locks after the first fill, preventing leakage during transport and tampering after delivery. The cap is large enough to accommodate a standard beverage dispenser nozzle, so that the employee can fill the box directly from the tea urn without a funnel. The box is **softer than a rigid plastic jug**, which allows it to be packed into a delivery bag without taking up excessive space and reduces the risk of cracking if the bag is bumped.

The economics of the half-gallon drink box are attractive. A half-gallon of sweet tea costs the store approximately **$0.35 to $0.50** in ingredients (tea bags, sugar, water, ice) and packaging. The retail price for a half-gallon is approximately **$4.99 to $6.99**, depending on the market. The gross margin is **85% to 90%**, which is higher than the margin on almost any food item on the menu. The box also increases the average delivery ticket, because a customer who orders a family meal is more likely to add a beverage when the beverage is presented as a natural part of the meal rather than an afterthought. A delivery order that includes a half-gallon of tea is a delivery order that is **larger, more profitable, and more likely to be repeated**.

The box should be offered in **three varieties**: Legendary Sweet Tea, Fresh Lemonade, and Limeade. The branding should be prominent and cheerful, with the Bojangles logo and a simple message: "Made fresh. Delivered cold. Enough for the whole table." The box is not a commodity container. It is a **brand ambassador** that sits on the customer's table and reminds them of the meal they just enjoyed.

## Chapter 7: The Delivery Workflow—From Order to Door

The Southern Love Delivery System workflow begins when the customer places an order through the Bojangles app, website, or phone line. The order is routed to the store's **delivery coordinator**, who is responsible for scheduling, preparation, and dispatch. The coordinator confirms the delivery address, the delivery window, and the contents of the order. If the order includes a half-gallon drink box, the coordinator ensures that the box is filled and sealed before the food is packed.

The food is packed in **insulated, vented containers** that preserve temperature and texture, as described in the operational volumes. The drink box is packed separately in a leak-proof bag or carrier. The entire order is assembled in a **delivery staging area** that is separate from the main counter, so that delivery orders do not compete with walk-in customers for space or staff attention. The staging area includes a **delivery checklist** that the coordinator uses to verify that every item is present, every container is sealed, and every label is correct.

The delivery is performed by a **Bojangles-employed driver** in a branded vehicle, or by a **dedicated third-party driver** who has been trained on Bojangles' standards. The driver follows a route that is optimized for the delivery window, and the customer receives a **text notification** when the driver is en route and when the order has been delivered. The driver confirms delivery through the app, and the customer receives a follow-up message asking for feedback. The feedback is reviewed by the store manager, and any issues are addressed within twenty-four hours.

The workflow is designed to be **repeatable and scalable**. A store that delivers ten orders per day can use the same workflow as a store that delivers fifty. The difference is the number of drivers and the size of the staging area, not the process itself. The process is the foundation of the Southern Love Delivery System, and it must be executed with the same discipline as the biscuit-making process. A delivery that arrives late or incomplete is a delivery that damages the brand, regardless of how good the food tastes.

# PART THREE: SALES ATTRIBUTES AND PRACTICES

## Chapter 8: The Attributes of a Bojangles Salesperson

The most successful salespeople in the Bojangles system share five attributes. They are **curious**, because they ask questions about the customer's needs rather than talking about the menu. They are **reliable**, because they do what they say they will do, when they say they will do it. They are **warm**, because they treat every customer like a neighbor rather than a target. They are **persistent**, because they follow up without being annoying. And they are **proud**, because they genuinely believe that the product they are selling is worth the customer's money.

Curiosity is the attribute that separates a salesperson from an order-taker. The curious salesperson asks, "What kind of meetings do you have? How many people usually attend? What have you ordered in the past that worked well?" The answers to these questions shape the proposal. A salesperson who knows that the customer's team meetings are small and early will propose a dozen biscuits and a coffee box, not a fifty-piece chicken platter. The proposal that fits the need is the proposal that gets accepted.

Reliability is the attribute that builds trust. The salesperson who promises a sample on Tuesday and delivers it on Tuesday is a salesperson who will be trusted with the monthly order. The salesperson who promises and forgets is a salesperson who will not be called again. Reliability is not glamorous. It is the **accumulation of small promises kept**. The salesperson who keeps the small promises earns the right to be trusted with the large ones.

Warmth is the attribute that makes the customer feel cared for. The warm salesperson remembers the customer's name, asks about their family, and congratulates them on their promotion. The warmth is not fake. It is the genuine interest in the customer as a person, not just as a source of revenue. The customer who feels known is a customer who is loyal. The customer who feels like a transaction is a customer who will switch to the competitor who offers a slightly lower price.

Persistence is the attribute that turns a maybe into a yes. The persistent salesperson follows up after the first sample, after the first order, and after the first month of silence. The persistence is not annoying because it is **timely and relevant**. The salesperson who calls two weeks before the customer's typical order date is not interrupting. They are anticipating. The persistence is the difference between a pipeline that dries up and a pipeline that flows.

Pride is the attribute that makes the sale authentic. The salesperson who genuinely believes that the Bojangles biscuit is the best biscuit in the market does not need to use high-pressure tactics. The pride is contagious. The customer who hears the pride in the salesperson's voice is a customer who wants to taste what the salesperson is talking about. The pride is the foundation of the sale.

## Chapter 9: The Practices That Build a Book of Business

The attributes are the foundation, but the practices are what turn the attributes into revenue. The first practice is **daily prospecting**. Ten contacts per day, every day, without exception. The contacts can be calls, walk-ins, or emails. The medium matters less than the consistency. The salesperson who makes ten contacts per day will build a pipeline. The salesperson who waits for the phone to ring will starve.

The second practice is **the sample delivery**. Every prospect should receive a sample before they are asked to order. The sample is not a gift. It is an **investment**. The sample should be delivered in person, with a business card and a menu. The sample should be followed up within one week. The sample is the most effective sales tool in the Bojangles arsenal because it allows the customer to taste the product before they commit. A customer who has tasted a fresh Bojangles biscuit is a customer who is halfway to the first order.

The third practice is **the reorder reminder**. Every recurring customer should receive a reminder two weeks before their typical order date. The reminder should be personal, not automated. A text message that says, "Hey [name], I know your team meeting is coming up. Want the same breakfast package as last time?" is worth more than a hundred email blasts. The reminder removes the friction from the customer's decision. The customer does not have to remember to order. The store has remembered for them.

The fourth practice is **the post-order follow-up**. After every catering order, the salesperson should call or text to confirm satisfaction. The follow-up is not a sales call. It is a **service call**. The customer who is asked "Was everything okay?" feels valued. The customer who is not asked feels like a transaction. The follow-up is also an opportunity to catch problems before they become complaints. A customer who had a minor issue with the order is a customer who can be saved by a quick apology and a small gesture of goodwill.

The fifth practice is **the referral ask**. Every satisfied customer should be asked for a referral. "Do you know anyone else in the building who might need catering?" The referral ask is the lowest-cost customer acquisition method available. The customer who refers a colleague is a customer who is **staking their reputation** on the Bojangles experience. The referral is the highest form of trust, and it should be treated as such. The salesperson who asks for referrals will build a book of business faster than the salesperson who relies on cold outreach alone.

# PART FOUR: MODERN SALES MEDIUMS AND MARKETING MATERIALS

## Chapter 10: The Sales Mediums That Matter in 2026

The salesperson in 2026 has more mediums available than at any point in history, but the mediums are not equally effective. The most effective medium for Bojangles corporate sales is still **the personal visit**. A salesperson who walks into an office with a dozen biscuits and a business card is more memorable than a hundred emails. The personal visit is expensive in time, but the conversion rate is higher than any other medium. The visit should be brief, warm, and focused on the sample. The goal is not to close the sale. The goal is to be remembered.

The second most effective medium is **the direct phone call**. The phone call is more personal than email and more efficient than a visit. The call should be brief and focused on a specific offer. "I'm calling because we're offering a breakfast package for offices in your area, and I wanted to see if you have a meeting coming up where it might be useful." The call should not be a script. It should be a conversation. The salesperson who listens more than they talk is the salesperson who gets the order.

The third medium is **text messaging**. Text is the preferred communication channel for many office managers and younger buyers. A text message that says, "Hey [name], it's [salesperson] from Bojangles. I'm putting together the catering schedule for next month. Do you want me to reserve your usual date?" is a low-friction way to maintain the relationship. The text should be used sparingly and respectfully. A text that is sent at 10 p.m. is a text that will be ignored. A text that is sent during business hours is a text that will be read.

The fourth medium is **email**. Email is the least personal of the primary mediums, but it is essential for **documentation and proposals**. The catering menu, the pricing, and the order confirmation should be sent by email so that the customer has a record. The email should be clean, professional, and easy to forward. The office manager who forwards the email to their boss is the office manager who is **selling on the salesperson's behalf**. The email should make that easy.

The fifth medium is **social media**, particularly for local marketing and fundraiser promotion. A store's social media account should feature photos of the food, the team, and the community events the store supports. The social media presence is not a direct sales channel. It is a **credibility channel**. The customer who checks the store's social media before ordering is a customer who wants to know that the store is real, active, and part of the community. The social media presence should be authentic and local, not corporate and generic.

## Chapter 11: Developing Marketing Materials That Work

The marketing materials for Bojangles corporate sales should be **simple, visual, and personal**. The most important piece is the **catering menu**. The menu should be a single page that can be printed, emailed, or posted on social media. It should feature photographs of the actual food—not stock photos, but photos of the store's biscuits and chicken. It should include the store manager's direct phone number. It should include a line that says, "Order by 5 p.m. for next-morning delivery." And it should include the **reorder promise**: "Same order, same time, one call."

The second piece is the **business card**. The business card is not a formality. It is the **physical artifact** that the customer keeps after the salesperson leaves. The card should include the salesperson's name, direct phone number, email, and the store address. It should include a simple line that communicates the value proposition: "Breakfast and lunch catering for teams and offices." The card should be sturdy and clean. A flimsy card is a card that gets thrown away. A sturdy card is a card that stays on the desk.

The third piece is the **sample delivery card**. When the salesperson delivers a sample, they should include a small card that says, "Enjoy the biscuits. If you need catering for a meeting, call me directly." The card should include the direct phone number. The card is the **bridge** between the sample and the first order. Without the card, the sample is just a free biscuit. With the card, the sample is a sales call.

The fourth piece is the **proposal template**. When the customer asks for a quote for a larger event, the salesperson should send a clean, one-page proposal that includes the items, the quantities, the price, the delivery time, and the payment terms. The proposal should be easy to read and easy to approve. The customer who has to decipher a complicated proposal is a customer who might give up and order from the competitor. The customer who receives a clear, professional proposal is a customer who feels confident in the decision.

The fifth piece is the **social media content calendar**. The store should post at least three times per week on its local social media accounts. The posts should include photos of the food, behind-the-scenes glimpses of the biscuit-making process, and announcements of community events and fundraisers. The calendar should be planned in advance so that the posts are consistent. The social media presence is not a replacement for personal sales. It is the **background music** that makes the personal sales more credible.

# PART FIVE: THE PHILOSOPHY OF SELLING BOJANGLES

## Chapter 12: Selling the Feeling, Not the Food

The customer who orders Bojangles catering is not just buying food. They are buying the **feeling of being taken care of**. The office manager who orders breakfast for a meeting is buying the feeling of being a hero to their team. The parent who orders a family meal is buying the feeling of a Sunday dinner without the work. The construction foreman who orders boxed lunches is buying the feeling of taking care of the crew. The food is the vehicle. The feeling is the product.

The salesperson who understands this is the salesperson who sells more. The salesperson who talks about the scratch-made biscuits and the hand-breaded chicken is selling features. The salesperson who talks about the meeting that will run smoothly, the team that will feel appreciated, and the family that will sit down together is selling **outcomes**. The outcomes are what the customer is buying. The features are the evidence that the outcomes are possible.

The Southern Love Delivery System is the physical expression of this philosophy. The half-gallon drink box is not just a container. It is a way of saying, "We know you are feeding a family, and we want to make sure everyone has enough." The delivery driver who arrives on time is not just a courier. They are the **embodiment of the brand's promise** that the customer will be taken care of. The follow-up call after the order is not just a service check. It is a way of saying, "We remember you, and we are glad you chose us."

## Chapter 13: The First-Mover Advantage in Sales

Bojangles is expanding into new markets—Oklahoma, Michigan, Brooklyn, Las Vegas—where the brand is not yet a household name . The salesperson in these markets has a unique advantage: **the novelty of the product**. A customer in Brooklyn who has never tasted a Bojangles biscuit is a customer who can be **delightfully surprised**. The first taste is the most powerful sales tool in the new market, because there is no preconceived notion to overcome. The biscuit speaks for itself.

The first-mover advantage also applies to the **automation story**. Bojangles is deploying Bo-Linda AI drive-thru across the entire system, with nearly 500 locations already live and a 98% success rate . The brand is investing in automated frying, automated side preparation, and automated quality monitoring. These investments are not just operational improvements. They are **sales talking points**. The customer who hears that Bojangles uses AI to ensure that every biscuit is perfect is a customer who trusts the brand more. The technology is the proof that the brand is serious about quality.

The salesperson should use the automation story carefully. The story is not "we have robots." The story is "we use technology to make sure that the food you receive is as good as the food we would serve our own family." The story is about **consistency and care**, not about efficiency. The customer who feels that the brand is using technology to take care of them is a customer who will pay a premium for the product. The customer who feels that the brand is using technology to cut costs is a customer who will shop on price.

## Chapter 14: The Long Game

Sales at Bojangles is not a short game. The account that orders breakfast for fifty people every month did not start with a fifty-person order. It started with a dozen biscuits and a business card. The relationship that generates $50,000 per year in catering revenue did not start with a proposal. It started with a conversation. The salesperson who understands the long game is the salesperson who invests in the small interactions that build the foundation for the large orders.

The long game requires **patience**. The prospect who does not order on the first call is not a lost prospect. They are a prospect who is not ready yet. The salesperson who follows up in two weeks, then in a month, then in a quarter, is the salesperson who will be there when the prospect is ready. The patience is not passive. It is **active persistence**. The salesperson who stays in touch without being annoying is the salesperson who wins the account when the competitor gives up.

The long game also requires **gratitude**. The customer who orders once should be thanked. The customer who orders repeatedly should be thanked repeatedly. The thank-you can be a handwritten note, a free dessert, or a simple text message. The gratitude is not a sales technique. It is a **human decency**. The customer who feels appreciated is a customer who will forgive a mistake, refer a colleague, and stay loyal through a price increase. The gratitude is the glue that holds the relationship together.

## Chapter 15: Conclusion—One Call, One Card, One Customer at a Time

The Bojangles corporate sales system is not complicated. It is a set of disciplines practiced consistently by people who believe in the product and care about the customer. The disciplines are: prospect every day, deliver samples, follow up promptly, ask for referrals, and thank the customer. The mediums are: personal visits, phone calls, texts, emails, and social media. The materials are: the catering menu, the business card, the sample card, the proposal, and the social media calendar. The philosophy is: sell the feeling, not the food.

The Southern Love Delivery System extends the philosophy to the customer's door. The half-gallon drink box is not just a container. It is a **statement of abundance**, a way of saying that Bojangles is the brand that feeds the whole family, not just the individual. The delivery driver is not just a courier. They are the **last mile of the brand promise**, the person who ensures that the food arrives hot, the tea arrives cold, and the customer feels taken care of.

The Main Ingredient Test applies to every sales call, every marketing piece, and every delivery: does this make the customer feel cared for, or merely sold to? The salesperson who passes the test is the salesperson who builds a book of business. The brand that passes the test is the brand that becomes a household name in every market it enters. The biscuit built Bojangles. The chicken made it famous. The sales system—one call at a time, one card at a time, one customer at a time—is what will make it **the brand that every community wants on its corner**.
