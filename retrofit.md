# RETROFITTING THE BOJANGLES BUFFET LINE: A Book on Automation, Refurbishment, and Energy-Efficient Heating

## Executive Summary

This book is a practical engineering and operations manual for retrofitting Bojangles' existing buffet service assembly—the front-line holding and display equipment described in earlier volumes—into an automated, energy-efficient, and maintainable system. The retrofit is not a replacement. It is an **upgrade of the existing booth front**, preserving the stainless steel housing, the pan rails, and the sneeze guard structure while installing new automation systems, automated lid-closing mechanisms, and advanced heating technologies that reduce electricity consumption. The book is organized into four parts. Part One establishes the retrofit philosophy and the assessment process. Part Two details the mechanical and electrical refurbishment, including the automated lid system. Part Three examines heating technologies and materials that reduce energy costs, with specific attention to induction, improved insulation, and smart control logic. Part Four integrates the data systems and concludes with a phased implementation plan. The governing principle remains the Main Ingredient Test: does this retrofit make the food hotter, the holding more reliable, or the operation more affordable without compromising quality? Every recommendation is evaluated against that standard.

# PART ONE: THE RETROFIT PHILOSOPHY AND ASSESSMENT

## Chapter 1: Why Retrofit Instead of Replace

The existing buffet assembly at a Bojangles location represents a substantial capital investment in stainless steel fabrication—the body frame, the pan rails, the water pans, the sneeze guard brackets, and the legs. These components do not degrade at the same rate as the electrical and control systems. The stainless steel, when properly maintained, can last fifteen to twenty years. The heating elements, thermostats, and wiring have a useful life of seven to ten years. The data and control systems have a useful life of three to five years. A retrofit replaces the short-lived components while preserving the long-lived structure, achieving 60% to 70% of the benefit of a new unit at 30% to 40% of the cost.

The environmental case for retrofit is equally compelling. Stainless steel production is energy-intensive and carbon-intensive. Discarding a functional stainless steel housing and replacing it with a new one generates embodied carbon that could be avoided by refurbishment. The retrofit approach aligns with the broader sustainability goals that restaurant chains are increasingly adopting, and it avoids the disruption of a full equipment replacement during operating hours. A retrofit can be performed in phases—overnight or during slow periods—while a full replacement requires the station to be out of service for days.

The strategic case for retrofit is that it preserves the **operational familiarity** of the existing equipment. Employees already know where the pans go, how the sneeze guard opens, and how the station is cleaned. A retrofit that preserves these interfaces while upgrading the internals reduces training burden and change resistance. The automated lid system, described in Chapter 4, is an addition rather than a replacement: the existing sneeze guard remains, but it gains a motorized closing mechanism that improves hygiene and reduces labor. The employees do not need to learn a new station; they need to learn a new feature of the station they already know.

## Chapter 2: The Retrofit Assessment—What to Keep, What to Replace, What to Add

The first step in any retrofit is a systematic assessment of the existing equipment. This assessment should be performed by the corporate engineering department or a qualified contractor, and it should produce a written report with photographs, measurements, and recommendations. The assessment covers four categories: **structural integrity, electrical condition, sanitation status, and automation readiness**.

Structural integrity assessment examines the stainless steel for corrosion, pitting, weld cracks, and deformation. The water pans are the most vulnerable component, because they are constantly wet and subjected to thermal cycling. A water pan with pinhole leaks or significant corrosion should be replaced, not repaired. The pan rails should be checked for straightness and for wear at the sliding surfaces. The legs should be checked for stability and for the condition of the sanitary feet. The sneeze guard brackets should be checked for security and for the smooth operation of any adjustable hinges.

Electrical condition assessment examines the heating elements, wiring, thermostats, and connections. The heating elements should be tested for resistance and for evidence of scale buildup or corrosion. The wiring should be inspected for insulation damage, overheating (discolored insulation), and loose connections. The thermostats should be tested against a calibrated reference thermometer. Any component that is more than seven years old or that shows signs of degradation should be replaced as part of the retrofit, even if it is still functioning, because the labor cost of accessing it later is higher than the component cost now.

Sanitation status assessment examines the food-contact surfaces for scratches, crevices, and areas that are difficult to clean. Stainless steel that has been scratched deeply can harbor bacteria and should be resurfaced or replaced. The corners and welds should be inspected for smoothness. The water pan drain should be checked for flow and for the condition of the valve. Any surface that cannot be cleaned to NSF standards should be addressed in the retrofit.

Automation readiness assessment examines the available space, power capacity, and data infrastructure. The retrofit will add sensors, a control board, a communication module, and possibly a motorized lid system. The electrical enclosure must have space for the new components, or a larger enclosure must be installed. The power supply must have capacity for the new loads (the lid motor and the control board are low-power, but the heating elements may be upgraded to higher-efficiency models that draw different current). The data infrastructure—Ethernet, Wi-Fi, or cellular—must be available at the station location.

## Chapter 3: The Refurbishment Process—Cleaning, Repair, and Recoating

The refurbishment of the stainless steel structure begins with a **deep clean** that removes grease, scale, and food residue from every surface. This is not the routine daily cleaning; it is a thorough degreasing with a commercial degreaser, followed by a descaling treatment for the water pans and heating elements. The degreaser should be applied with a brush or a low-pressure spray, allowed to dwell for the recommended time, and rinsed thoroughly. The descaler (typically a food-safe citric or phosphoric acid solution) should be circulated through the water pan and the drain to remove mineral deposits.

After cleaning, the structure is inspected for **damage that was hidden by grease or scale**. Welds that appeared sound may reveal cracks once the scale is removed. Corrosion that was masked by discoloration may be more extensive than expected. Any damage found during this inspection must be repaired before the automation components are installed. Small cracks can be re-welded and ground smooth. Pitting corrosion can be treated with a food-safe epoxy if it is not in a high-wear area, or the affected panel can be replaced. Extensive corrosion in the water pan is a reason to replace the pan rather than repair it.

The **recoating** step is optional but recommended for the exterior surfaces. The original brushed stainless steel finish can be restored with a non-abrasive polishing compound and a soft cloth. If the exterior has been painted or coated, the coating should be inspected for peeling or chipping and repaired or removed. A clean, polished exterior communicates cleanliness to the customer and makes the equipment easier to wipe down during daily cleaning. The interior food-contact surfaces should not be coated; they should be left as bare stainless steel, passivated if necessary to restore corrosion resistance.

The **reassembly** of the mechanical components follows the cleaning and repair. New gaskets and seals should be installed on the water pan drain, the pan rails, and any access panels. The legs should be replaced if they are unstable or if the sanitary feet are worn. The sneeze guard panels should be inspected for scratches and replaced if they are visibly damaged, because scratched glass or polycarbonate is difficult to clean and looks unhygienic even when it is clean.

# PART TWO: THE AUTOMATED LID SYSTEM AND ELECTRICAL UPGRADES

## Chapter 4: The Automated Lid-Closing Mechanism—Design and Integration

The automated lid-closing system is the most visible automation feature of the retrofit. It addresses a persistent operational problem: the sneeze guard or lid is left open by customers or employees, exposing the food to contamination and allowing heat to escape. A lid that closes automatically after each serving reduces contamination risk, conserves heat, and presents a tidier appearance to the dining room. The system is not a replacement for the existing sneeze guard; it is a **motorized actuator and control module** that is added to the existing hinge or sliding mechanism.

The design of the automated lid system depends on the existing sneeze guard configuration. If the guard is a **hinged glass panel** that lifts upward, the automation uses a **linear actuator or a rotary motor with a linkage** that connects the motor to the panel. The motor is mounted on the existing stainless steel bracket, and the linkage is designed to lift the panel smoothly without binding. If the guard is a **sliding panel**, the automation uses a **belt drive or a rack-and-pinion mechanism** that moves the panel along its existing track. In both cases, the motor is a **low-voltage DC gearmotor** (12V or 24V) with an integrated encoder that provides position feedback.

The control logic for the automated lid is simple but must be robust. The lid opens when a customer approaches (detected by a **proximity sensor or a capacitive touch strip** on the front of the station) or when an employee activates a manual open button. The lid closes automatically after a **programmable delay** (typically five to ten seconds) once the customer moves away or after the serving is complete. The lid must also close automatically if the station is left unattended for an extended period, and it must open fully for cleaning and pan replacement. A **safety clutch or a current-limiting circuit** prevents the motor from crushing a hand or an object if the lid encounters resistance during closing. The motor stops and reverses if the current exceeds a threshold, and an audible alert sounds.

The integration with the existing structure requires careful attention to the **hinge geometry and the weight of the panel**. A glass sneeze guard panel can weigh five to fifteen pounds, depending on its size. The motor must be sized to lift this weight with a margin of safety, and the linkage must be designed so that the panel does not swing or slam. The motor should be mounted so that it is protected from water and cleaning chemicals, ideally inside the electrical enclosure or in a sealed housing attached to the frame. The wiring between the motor and the control board should be routed through the existing cable management or through new conduit.

## Chapter 5: Electrical System Upgrades—Wiring, Safety, and Controls

The electrical system of the existing buffet assembly is likely adequate for the original heating elements and thermostats, but it may not be adequate for the retrofit. The addition of the control board, the sensors, the communication module, and the lid motor requires **additional low-voltage circuits** and may require a **larger electrical enclosure**. The retrofit should include a full inspection and upgrade of the wiring, with the following minimum requirements.

All wiring should be **stranded copper** with insulation rated for the temperature and moisture environment. Solid wire is acceptable for static connections inside the enclosure, but stranded wire is preferred for connections that may be subject to vibration or movement. The wire gauge should be sized for the current load with a margin of 25% to account for future additions. The wiring should be **color-coded** (black for line voltage, red for low-voltage DC, white for neutral, green for ground) and should be **labeled at both ends** with a wire marker or a printed label.

The electrical enclosure should be upgraded to **NEMA 4 or NEMA 4X** if it is not already, to protect the new control components from water and cleaning chemicals. The enclosure should have a **din rail** for mounting the control board, relays, and terminal blocks, and it should have **knockouts or cable glands** for the sensor and motor cables. The enclosure should be grounded to the frame with a dedicated grounding conductor, and all metal parts of the station should be bonded to the same ground point.

The **control board** described in the previous volume—the U.S.-designed microprocessor board with sensor inputs and communication outputs—is installed in the upgraded enclosure. The board receives power from a **24V DC power supply** that is also mounted in the enclosure. The power supply should be sized to provide at least 50% headroom above the calculated load of the board, the sensors, and the lid motor. The board connects to the existing thermostats through **dry contact interfaces** or through **analog inputs** that read the thermostat set point and actual temperature. The board does not replace the thermostats; it **monitors** them and **overrides** them only when necessary (for example, to shut off the heating element if the low-water cutoff is triggered).

The **safety systems** of the retrofit must be designed to fail safe. If the control board fails, the existing thermostats must continue to regulate the heating elements, and the station must remain operational at a basic level. If the lid motor fails, the lid must be able to be opened and closed manually. If the power fails, the lid should remain in its current position, not fall open or closed. These fail-safe requirements are achieved through **mechanical overrides** (a manual release for the lid motor) and **electrical separation** (the control board is on a separate circuit from the heating elements, so a board failure does not affect heating).

# PART THREE: ENERGY-EFFICIENT HEATING TECHNOLOGIES AND MATERIALS

## Chapter 6: The Economics of Electricity in Commercial Food Holding

Electricity is the largest operating cost of a buffet holding station after labor. A typical six-pan electric steam table draws approximately 3,000 to 4,000 watts during operation, and if it runs for twelve hours per day, it consumes 36 to 48 kWh per day. At a commercial electricity rate of $0.12 to $0.18 per kWh, the annual cost is $1,600 to $3,100 per station. A Bojangles with two buffet stations could spend $3,200 to $6,200 per year on electricity for holding alone. These figures do not include the cost of the electricity used to heat the water in the water pan, which is the primary energy sink in wet-operation steam tables.

The energy efficiency of a steam table is inherently low. The water pan must be heated to near-boiling, and a significant portion of that heat is lost to the surrounding air through the water surface, the pan walls, and the sneeze guard opening. The heating elements cycle on and off to maintain the set point, but they are sized for recovery from cold, not for steady-state efficiency. A well-designed retrofit can reduce the electricity consumption of the station by **20% to 40%** through a combination of improved heating technology, better insulation, and smarter control logic. The payback period for these improvements is typically two to four years, depending on the local electricity rate and the hours of operation.

## Chapter 7: Induction Heating for Buffet Holding

Induction heating is the most significant energy-efficiency improvement available for buffet holding. Unlike resistive heating, which heats a element that then transfers heat to the water or the pan by conduction, induction heating uses an **oscillating magnetic field** to induce eddy currents directly in the ferromagnetic base of the pan. The pan itself becomes the heating element, and the heat is generated exactly where it is needed—in the food container—rather than in a separate element that must transfer heat through air or water.

The efficiency advantage of induction is substantial. A resistive steam table transfers approximately 60% to 70% of the electrical energy into the water bath, with the remainder lost to the enclosure and the surrounding air. An induction system transfers **85% to 90%** of the electrical energy directly into the pan, because there is no intermediate heating element and no water bath to heat. The pan reaches temperature faster, the energy is more precisely controlled, and the station can be turned off or down when not in use without the thermal lag of a water bath. The elimination of the water bath also eliminates the water consumption and the scale buildup that requires descaling maintenance.

The implementation of induction in a retrofit requires that the existing pans be replaced with **induction-compatible pans**—pans with a ferromagnetic base, typically stainless steel with a magnetic layer or a clad construction. Standard 304 stainless steel pans are not magnetic and will not work with induction. The retrofit kit should include a set of induction-compatible pans in the standard sizes (full-size, half-size, third-size) and should specify that the pans must not be replaced with non-compatible pans. The induction coils are mounted beneath the pan rails, replacing the resistive heating elements and the water pan. The control board manages the induction power modules, which require **higher-frequency switching circuits** and **filtering** to prevent electrical interference with other equipment. The induction system should be specified with **power factor correction** to present a resistive load to the building's electrical system and avoid penalties from the utility.

The energy savings from induction are most significant in stations that operate for long hours with variable demand. A resistive steam table must be kept hot all day because the water bath takes time to heat up. An induction system can be turned down or off during slow periods and brought back to temperature in minutes, because there is no water bath to heat. The control logic can use **demand-based modulation**: the station holds at a lower temperature (for example, 145°F instead of 160°F) during slow periods and raises the temperature when a customer approaches or when the POS indicates an order is incoming. This modulation is not possible with a water bath, which has too much thermal mass to respond quickly.

## Chapter 8: Insulation and Material Improvements

The enclosure of the buffet station is a significant source of heat loss. The stainless steel walls conduct heat from the interior to the exterior, and the exterior surfaces radiate that heat into the dining room, increasing the air conditioning load. The retrofit should include **insulation** on the interior surfaces of the enclosure, behind the pan rails and beneath the water pan (or the induction coils). The insulation should be a **closed-cell foam or aerogel blanket** with a high R-value per inch, a Class A fire rating, and a foil facing that reflects radiant heat back toward the food. The insulation must be sealed against moisture, because wet insulation loses its insulating value and can become a sanitation problem.

The **sneeze guard material** also affects energy consumption. A glass sneeze guard is transparent but allows radiant heat to escape. A **low-emissivity (low-E) coated glass** reflects infrared radiation back toward the food while remaining transparent in the visible spectrum. The low-E coating reduces radiant heat loss by 30% to 50% compared to uncoated glass. The coating is applied to the interior surface of the glass (the side facing the food), and it is durable enough to withstand cleaning. The cost premium for low-E glass is modest—approximately 15% to 25% above standard tempered glass—and the energy savings justify the investment in stations that operate for extended hours.

The **pan material** is another variable. Standard stainless steel pans conduct heat reasonably well, but they also radiate heat from their exposed surfaces. **Clad pans** with an aluminum core and stainless steel exterior conduct heat more evenly and allow the induction system to operate at lower power settings. The pans should be **matched to the induction coil size** so that the magnetic field couples efficiently. A pan that is too small for the coil wastes energy; a pan that is too large may not heat evenly. The retrofit kit should specify the pan sizes that are compatible with the installed coil configuration.

## Chapter 9: Smart Control Logic and Demand-Based Heating

The control board described in the previous volume is the foundation for **demand-based heating**, which is the most cost-effective energy-saving strategy because it requires no new hardware beyond what the retrofit already includes. The logic is straightforward: the station does not need to be at full serving temperature when no customers are present. It needs to be at full temperature when a customer is approaching or when an order is imminent. The control board uses data from three sources to predict demand.

The first source is the **proximity sensor** on the front of the station. When a customer approaches within a defined distance (for example, three feet), the control board raises the set point to serving temperature. The station is already at a holding temperature (for example, 145°F), so the increase to 160°F takes only a few minutes with induction heating. The second source is the **POS system**. When an order is placed that includes a side from the buffet, the control board receives a signal and raises the set point for the relevant pan. The third source is **historical traffic data**. The control board learns the typical customer arrival patterns by day of week and time of day, and it pre-heats the station in anticipation of peak periods. During slow periods, it allows the temperature to drift down to the holding set point.

The energy savings from demand-based heating depend on the traffic pattern. A station that is busy for four hours and slow for eight hours can save 15% to 25% of its energy by holding at a lower temperature during the slow period and heating up only when needed. The savings are greater with induction than with a water bath, because the induction system responds quickly and does not have the thermal inertia of water. The control logic should be **transparent and overrideable**: the store manager can force the station to full temperature for a special event or a rush, and the system should log the override so that the energy impact can be analyzed.

# PART FOUR: DATA INTEGRATION AND PHASED IMPLEMENTATION

## Chapter 10: Integrating the Retrofit with the Bojangles Data Platform

The retrofit is not complete until the upgraded station is connected to the Bojangles data platform. The control board publishes temperature data, energy consumption data, lid status, and alert history to the cloud platform described in the previous volume. The platform aggregates this data across all retrofitted stations, allowing corporate engineering to compare the energy performance of different retrofit configurations and to identify opportunities for further improvement.

The **energy data** is particularly valuable. The control board can measure the power consumption of each heating zone by monitoring the current draw through a **current transformer (CT)** on the power line. The platform calculates the energy cost per station, per store, and per region, and it flags stations that are consuming more energy than comparable stations. A station with an unusually high energy consumption may have a failing heating element, a misconfigured control setting, or a lid that is not closing properly. The data makes the invisible visible, and it allows the engineering department to prioritize maintenance and further retrofits.

The **temperature data** serves the food safety compliance function described in the previous volume. The platform generates HACCP-compliant logs for each station, with time-stamped temperature readings and alerts for any deviation from the safe range. The store manager reviews the logs daily, and the corporate food safety team reviews them weekly. The logs are exportable for health inspections, and the platform retains them for the required period.

## Chapter 11: The Phased Retrofit Plan

The retrofit should be implemented in three phases, each with a defined scope, timeline, and budget. **Phase One** (months 1–3) is the pilot: two to four stores in different markets are selected for a full retrofit, including the automated lid, the electrical upgrades, the induction heating, and the data integration. The pilot stores are instrumented to collect detailed energy and performance data, and the results are compared to a control group of non-retrofitted stores. The pilot is the proving ground: if the energy savings or the operational improvements do not meet expectations, the design is adjusted before broader rollout.

**Phase Two** (months 4–12) is the regional rollout: the retrofit is deployed to 20% to 30% of stores, prioritized by energy cost (stores in high-electricity-rate markets first) and by equipment age (stations that are oldest and most likely to fail first). The regional rollout is managed by a dedicated retrofit team that includes engineering, operations, and a contracted installation crew. Each retrofit takes one to two days per station, performed overnight to minimize disruption. The data from the regional rollout is analyzed to refine the installation process and the equipment specifications for the final phase.

**Phase Three** (months 13–24) is the system-wide rollout: the remaining stores are retrofitted according to the refined process. The system-wide rollout is managed through a **retrofit kit** that contains all the components required for a standard installation, pre-assembled and tested at the factory where possible. The kit approach reduces installation time, ensures consistency across stores, and simplifies inventory management. The kit includes the control board, the sensors, the lid motor and linkage, the induction coils (if the induction option is selected), the insulation, and the wiring harness. The installation crew follows a standardized procedure, and the completed station is tested and commissioned before the crew leaves the site.

## Chapter 12: Conclusion—The Retrofit as a Statement of Priorities

The retrofit of the Bojangles buffet assembly is not a glamorous project. It does not produce a new menu item or a new store format. It does not generate headlines or social media excitement. But it is a statement of priorities. It says that Bojangles cares about the food that sits in the holding station—that it is worth keeping hot, worth keeping safe, and worth presenting well. It says that the company cares about the electricity bill that the franchisee pays every month—that efficiency is a form of respect for the operator's investment. And it says that the company cares about the employees who work the buffet line—that their job should be about serving customers, not about manually closing lids and adjusting thermostats.

The Main Ingredient Test applies to every element of the retrofit. The automated lid keeps the food hotter and safer. The induction heating keeps the food at temperature with less energy and less water. The insulation keeps the heat where it belongs. The smart control logic matches energy use to actual demand. The data integration proves that the food was held safely and shows where energy can be saved. Each element passes the test. Each element belongs. The retrofit is not the most exciting project in this book, but it may be the one that delivers the most consistent return—month after month, store after store, in the form of lower bills, better food, and a station that works the way it should.
