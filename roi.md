# Comprehensive Simulated ROI Analysis: Bojangles Automation and Equipment Upgrade Program

## Executive Summary

This document presents a comprehensive simulated return on investment (ROI) analysis for the Bojangles automation and equipment upgrade program described throughout this book series. The analysis covers a **single store**, a **group of 10 stores**, and a **system-wide rollout of 100 stores**, with a full breakdown of capital costs, operating savings, revenue uplift, and payback periods. The simulation uses conservative assumptions grounded in the manufacturer pricing data, labor rates, and oil consumption figures documented in previous volumes. The base case yields a **3.0-year payback** for a single mid-volume store, a **2.7-year blended payback** for a 10-store group with volume discounts, and a **2.5-year payback** for a 100-store system-wide rollout with mature installation processes. The internal rate of return (IRR) ranges from **28% to 38%** depending on the scenario, and the net present value (NPV) over a five-year horizon is positive in every scenario. The analysis includes sensitivity testing for oil prices, labor rates, store volume, and capital cost overruns, and it concludes that the investment is robust across a wide range of plausible conditions.

## Part One: Simulation Assumptions and Inputs

### 1.1 Store Profile Assumptions

The simulation uses three store profiles to capture the variability across the Bojangles system. The **high-volume store** generates approximately $2.5 million in annual sales, which aligns with the mean unit volume reported in the 2026 Franchise Disclosure Document. The **mid-volume store** generates approximately $2.1 million, which aligns with the median unit volume. The **low-volume store** generates approximately $1.6 million, representing the lower quartile of the system. Each profile assumes a breakfast-heavy sales mix (40% breakfast, 35% lunch, 25% dinner), consistent with the brand's historical daypart distribution in core markets.

The **food cost** is assumed at 32% of sales, consistent with the brand's overall margin structure. The **labor cost** is assumed at 28% of sales, with a fully loaded hourly rate of $18 per hour for the mid-volume store, $22 per hour for the high-volume store in a high-wage market, and $15 per hour for the low-volume store in a low-wage market. The **oil consumption** is assumed at 100 pounds per week for the mid-volume store, 130 pounds per week for the high-volume store, and 75 pounds per week for the low-volume store, based on the frying volume implied by the sales mix.

### 1.2 Capital Cost Assumptions

The capital cost assumptions are drawn directly from the manufacturer pricing and component estimates in previous volumes. The **fryer battery** (dual-vat or triple-vat with automatic filtration, automatic top-off, and waste oil interface) is estimated at $42,000 for the mid-volume store, $50,000 for the high-volume store (triple-vat), and $35,000 for the low-volume store (dual-vat with fewer automation options). The **side preparation modules** are estimated at $32,000 for the mid-volume store, $40,000 for the high-volume store, and $25,000 for the low-volume store. The **service-line holding and display equipment** (retrofit or new buffet assembly with automated lids and induction heating) is estimated at $20,000 for the mid-volume store, $25,000 for the high-volume store, and $15,000 for the low-volume store. The **data system** (control boards, sensors, communication modules, cloud integration) is estimated at $10,000 for all profiles. The **pickup infrastructure** (heated lockers or secure shelving, POS integration) is estimated at $10,000 for the mid-volume store, $15,000 for the high-volume store, and $5,000 for the low-volume store. The **installation and commissioning** is estimated at $6,500 for the mid-volume store, $8,000 for the high-volume store, and $5,000 for the low-volume store.

The **total capital cost** is therefore approximately **$120,500** for the mid-volume store, **$148,000** for the high-volume store, and **$90,000** for the low-volume store. These figures exclude building modifications (electrical upgrades, floor reinforcement, drain installation), which are estimated at an additional $15,000 for stores that require them. The simulation assumes that 60% of stores require some building modification, with an average cost of $15,000, adding an expected $9,000 to the per-store capital cost. The **all-in capital cost** is therefore approximately **$129,500** for the mid-volume store, **$157,000** for the high-volume store, and **$99,000** for the low-volume store.

### 1.3 Savings Driver Assumptions

The **oil savings** are assumed at 40% of baseline oil spend, based on Henny Penny's Evolution Elite and Frymaster's FilterQuick 30 both claiming 40% oil reduction, and Pitco's ROV claiming up to 50% with the SmartOIL sensor. The simulation uses 40% as the base case and tests 30% and 50% in the sensitivity analysis. The baseline oil spend is calculated as oil consumption (pounds per week) × 52 weeks × oil price per pound. The oil price is assumed at $1.75 per pound for the base case, with sensitivity testing at $1.40 and $2.10.

The **labor savings** are assumed at 0.75 FTE per store for the mid-volume profile, 1.0 FTE for the high-volume profile, and 0.5 FTE for the low-volume profile. The savings come from automated filtration (eliminating manual filtering labor), automated side preparation (reducing cooking labor), and automated holding-time management (reducing monitoring labor). The labor savings are valued at the fully loaded hourly rate for each profile, multiplied by 2,080 hours per year. The simulation assumes that 60% of the labor savings are realized as actual cost reduction (through reduced hours or avoided hires) and 40% are redeployed to customer service, which is treated as a revenue-enabling benefit rather than a cost reduction.

The **waste reduction** is assumed at 3% of food cost, based on the holding-time management and demand-based production capabilities. For the mid-volume store with $672,000 in annual food cost (32% of $2.1 million), the waste reduction is approximately $20,160 per year. The **energy savings** are assumed at $2,000 per year for the mid-volume store, $2,500 for the high-volume store, and $1,500 for the low-volume store, based on the induction heating and insulation improvements described in the retrofit volume.

The **revenue uplift** is assumed at 1.5% of sales for the mid-volume store, 2.0% for the high-volume store, and 1.0% for the low-volume store. The uplift comes from improved speed of service, better product consistency, and the new menu items enabled by the automation (Country Fried Steak Dinner, new breadings, new sauces). The simulation applies a **50% flow-through** to the revenue uplift, meaning that half of the incremental revenue becomes incremental profit, and half is consumed by the variable costs of serving the additional customers.

## Part Two: Single-Store ROI Simulation

### 2.1 Mid-Volume Store Base Case

The mid-volume store is the representative case for the system. The annual savings and benefits are calculated as follows. Oil savings: 100 lbs/week × 52 weeks × $1.75/lb × 40% = **$3,640**. Labor savings: 0.75 FTE × $18/hr × 2,080 hrs × 60% realization = **$16,848**. Waste reduction: $672,000 × 3% = **$20,160**. Energy savings: **$2,000**. Revenue uplift profit: $2.1 million × 1.5% × 50% flow-through = **$15,750**. The **total annual benefit** is **$58,398**.

The **total capital cost** is **$129,500**. The **simple payback period** is $129,500 ÷ $58,398 = **2.22 years**. The **five-year net cash flow** is ($58,398 × 5) − $129,500 = **$162,490**. The **five-year ROI** is $162,490 ÷ $129,500 = **125%**. The **IRR** over five years is approximately **38%**.

### 2.2 High-Volume Store Base Case

The high-volume store benefits from higher sales, higher labor rates, and greater oil consumption. Oil savings: 130 lbs/week × 52 weeks × $1.75/lb × 40% = **$4,732**. Labor savings: 1.0 FTE × $22/hr × 2,080 hrs × 60% realization = **$27,456**. Waste reduction: $800,000 × 3% = **$24,000**. Energy savings: **$2,500**. Revenue uplift profit: $2.5 million × 2.0% × 50% = **$25,000**. The **total annual benefit** is **$83,688**.

The **total capital cost** is **$157,000**. The **simple payback period** is $157,000 ÷ $83,688 = **1.88 years**. The **five-year net cash flow** is ($83,688 × 5) − $157,000 = **$261,440**. The **five-year ROI** is $261,440 ÷ $157,000 = **167%**. The **IRR** over five years is approximately **48%**.

### 2.3 Low-Volume Store Base Case

The low-volume store has lower sales and lower labor rates, which reduces the absolute savings but also reduces the capital cost. Oil savings: 75 lbs/week × 52 weeks × $1.75/lb × 40% = **$2,730**. Labor savings: 0.5 FTE × $15/hr × 2,080 hrs × 60% realization = **$9,360**. Waste reduction: $512,000 × 3% = **$15,360**. Energy savings: **$1,500**. Revenue uplift profit: $1.6 million × 1.0% × 50% = **$8,000**. The **total annual benefit** is **$36,950**.

The **total capital cost** is **$99,000**. The **simple payback period** is $99,000 ÷ $36,950 = **2.68 years**. The **five-year net cash flow** is ($36,950 × 5) − $99,000 = **$85,750**. The **five-year ROI** is $85,750 ÷ $99,000 = **87%**. The **IRR** over five years is approximately **28%**.

## Part Three: Group-Level ROI Simulation

### 3.1 Ten-Store Group

The 10-store group is assumed to have a mix of three high-volume stores, five mid-volume stores, and two low-volume stores. The group benefits from **volume purchasing discounts** of 7% on the fryer batteries, side preparation modules, and holding equipment, and 5% on the data systems and pickup infrastructure. The installation cost is reduced by 10% as the installation crew gains experience across the stores.

The **total capital cost** for the group, before discounts, is (3 × $157,000) + (5 × $129,500) + (2 × $99,000) = **$1,317,500**. After the 7% discount on equipment and the 10% discount on installation, the **discounted capital cost** is approximately **$1,225,000**. The **total annual benefit** for the group, before any group-level synergies, is (3 × $83,688) + (5 × $58,398) + (2 × $36,950) = **$616,864**. The group also realizes a **shared services benefit** of $25,000 per year from centralized data monitoring, which reduces the need for per-store management attention.

The **total annual benefit** for the group is **$641,864**. The **simple payback period** is $1,225,000 ÷ $641,864 = **1.91 years**. The **five-year net cash flow** is ($641,864 × 5) − $1,225,000 = **$1,984,320**. The **five-year ROI** is $1,984,320 ÷ $1,225,000 = **162%**. The **IRR** over five years is approximately **46%**.

### 3.2 One Hundred-Store System-Wide Rollout

The 100-store rollout is assumed to have the same mix of store profiles as the 10-store group (30 high-volume, 50 mid-volume, 20 low-volume). The rollout benefits from **mature installation processes** that reduce installation cost by 15% compared to the 10-store group, and from **volume purchasing discounts** of 12% on equipment and 8% on data systems. The rollout also benefits from **operational learning** that increases the labor savings realization rate from 60% to 70%, as store managers become more adept at redeploying labor to customer service and as the automation systems are refined based on field data.

The **total capital cost** for the rollout, before discounts, is (30 × $157,000) + (50 × $129,500) + (20 × $99,000) = **$13,175,000**. After the volume discounts and the installation efficiency, the **discounted capital cost** is approximately **$11,600,000**. The **total annual benefit** for the rollout, with the higher labor realization rate and the shared services benefit of $250,000 per year, is approximately **$6,800,000**.

The **simple payback period** is $11,600,000 ÷ $6,800,000 = **1.71 years**. The **five-year net cash flow** is ($6,800,000 × 5) − $11,600,000 = **$22,400,000**. The **five-year ROI** is $22,400,000 ÷ $11,600,000 = **193%**. The **IRR** over five years is approximately **52%**.

## Part Four: Sensitivity Analysis and Risk Assessment

### 4.1 Oil Price Sensitivity

The oil price is the most volatile input in the model. The base case assumes $1.75 per pound. A **20% increase** to $2.10 per pound increases the oil savings by 20%, which adds approximately $728 per year for the mid-volume store. This reduces the payback period from 2.22 to 2.19 years. A **20% decrease** to $1.40 per pound reduces the oil savings by 20%, which subtracts approximately $728 per year, extending the payback to 2.25 years. The payback period is **not highly sensitive to oil price** because oil savings are a relatively small portion of the total benefit (approximately 6% for the mid-volume store). The labor savings and waste reduction are the dominant drivers.

### 4.2 Labor Rate Sensitivity

The labor rate is the most impactful variable in the model. A **$2 per hour increase** in the fully loaded labor rate (from $18 to $20 for the mid-volume store) increases the labor savings by approximately $1,872 per year (0.75 FTE × $2/hr × 2,080 hrs × 60% realization). This reduces the payback period from 2.22 to 2.15 years. A **$2 per hour decrease** (from $18 to $16) reduces the labor savings by the same amount, extending the payback to 2.30 years. The payback period is **moderately sensitive to labor rate**, but the investment remains attractive across the plausible range.

### 4.3 Store Volume Sensitivity

A **10% decrease** in store volume (from $2.1 million to $1.89 million) reduces the waste reduction and revenue uplift proportionally, and may also reduce the labor savings if the store reduces staffing. The total annual benefit for the mid-volume store would decrease from $58,398 to approximately $52,000, extending the payback from 2.22 to 2.49 years. A **10% increase** in store volume would increase the benefit to approximately $64,800, reducing the payback to 2.00 years. The investment remains attractive across a ±10% volume range, but stores with volumes below $1.5 million may have payback periods exceeding 3.5 years, which is at the upper end of the acceptable range.

### 4.4 Capital Cost Overrun Sensitivity

A **15% capital cost overrun** (from $129,500 to $148,925 for the mid-volume store) extends the payback from 2.22 to 2.55 years. A **25% overrun** extends it to 2.77 years. The investment remains attractive even with a 25% overrun, but the project management should include a contingency of 10% to 15% to absorb unexpected costs without eroding the ROI.

### 4.5 Combined Downside Scenario

The **combined downside scenario** assumes a 15% capital cost overrun, a 10% volume decrease, a $2 per hour labor rate decrease, and a 20% oil price decrease. Under these conditions, the mid-volume store's total annual benefit decreases to approximately $44,000, and the capital cost increases to $148,925. The payback period extends to **3.38 years**, which is still within the acceptable range but leaves less margin for error. This scenario should be used as the **stress test** for the investment decision: if the project can survive the combined downside, it is robust enough to proceed.

## Part Five: Five-Year Financial Projection and NPV

### 5.1 Mid-Volume Store Five-Year Cash Flow

The five-year cash flow projection for the mid-volume store, using the base case assumptions, is as follows. Year 0: −$129,500 (capital investment). Year 1: +$58,398. Year 2: +$58,398. Year 3: +$58,398. Year 4: +$58,398. Year 5: +$58,398. The **cumulative cash flow** at the end of Year 5 is **$162,490**. The **NPV** at a 10% discount rate is calculated as: −$129,500 + ($58,398 ÷ 1.10) + ($58,398 ÷ 1.10²) + ($58,398 ÷ 1.10³) + ($58,398 ÷ 1.10⁴) + ($58,398 ÷ 1.10⁵) = **$91,850**. The **discounted payback period** is approximately **2.7 years**.

### 5.2 Ten-Store Group Five-Year Cash Flow

The 10-store group's five-year cash flow, using the discounted capital cost of $1,225,000 and the annual benefit of $641,864, is as follows. Year 0: −$1,225,000. Years 1–5: +$641,864 per year. The **cumulative cash flow** at the end of Year 5 is **$1,984,320**. The **NPV** at 10% is **$1,007,700**. The **discounted payback period** is approximately **2.3 years**.

### 5.3 One Hundred-Store Rollout Five-Year Cash Flow

The 100-store rollout's five-year cash flow, using the discounted capital cost of $11,600,000 and the annual benefit of $6,800,000, is as follows. Year 0: −$11,600,000. Years 1–5: +$6,800,000 per year. The **cumulative cash flow** at the end of Year 5 is **$22,400,000**. The **NPV** at 10% is **$14,170,000**. The **discounted payback period** is approximately **2.0 years**.

### 5.4 Terminal Value Considerations

The five-year projection does not include the **terminal value** of the equipment, which has a useful life of seven to ten years for the fryer and holding equipment, and three to five years for the data systems. If the equipment is assumed to have a residual value of 20% of capital cost at the end of Year 5 (approximately $25,900 for the mid-volume store, $245,000 for the 10-store group, and $2,320,000 for the 100-store rollout), the five-year NPV increases accordingly. The terminal value should be treated conservatively in the investment decision, but it provides additional upside that is not captured in the base case.

## Part Six: Qualitative Benefits and Strategic Value

The ROI simulation captures the **quantifiable financial benefits** of the automation program, but there are qualitative benefits that are not included in the numbers and that may be equally important to the long-term health of the brand. The **consistency of product quality** improves when the fryer and the side preparation modules execute the same program every time, regardless of which employee is working. The **food safety compliance** improves when the data system automatically logs temperatures and generates HACCP records, reducing the risk of a health inspection failure or a foodborne illness incident. The **employee safety** improves when the automated oil disposal eliminates the burn risk from hot oil handling, reducing workers' compensation claims and improving morale. The **customer experience** improves when the order-ahead pickup is reliable, the sides are fresh, and the chicken is consistently golden and crispy.

These qualitative benefits are difficult to quantify, but they have a direct impact on **customer retention and brand equity**. A customer who receives a consistently good product is more likely to return, and a customer who returns is worth more over their lifetime than a customer who visits once. The **lifetime value** of a loyal Bojangles customer is estimated at $2,000 to $5,000, depending on visit frequency and average ticket. A 1% improvement in retention across the system could be worth millions of dollars in incremental lifetime value, which dwarfs the capital cost of the automation program. The ROI simulation does not include this benefit because it is difficult to attribute precisely, but it should be considered in the strategic decision.

## Conclusion

The comprehensive simulated ROI analysis demonstrates that the Bojangles automation and equipment upgrade program is financially attractive across all store profiles and all scenarios tested. The **mid-volume store** achieves a 2.22-year payback, a 125% five-year ROI, and a 38% IRR. The **high-volume store** achieves a 1.88-year payback, a 167% five-year ROI, and a 48% IRR. The **low-volume store** achieves a 2.68-year payback, an 87% five-year ROI, and a 28% IRR. The **10-store group** achieves a 1.91-year payback, a 162% five-year ROI, and a 46% IRR. The **100-store rollout** achieves a 1.71-year payback, a 193% five-year ROI, and a 52% IRR. The **combined downside scenario** extends the mid-volume payback to 3.38 years, which is still within the acceptable range.

The investment is **robust to oil price fluctuations, labor rate changes, store volume variations, and capital cost overruns**. The most sensitive variable is the labor rate, because labor savings are the largest single benefit, but the investment remains attractive even with a $2 per hour decrease in the labor rate. The **NPV is positive in every scenario** at a 10% discount rate, and the **discounted payback period is under three years** for the mid-volume store and under 2.5 years for the group and system-wide scenarios.

The Main Ingredient Test applies to the financial analysis as it does to every other element of this plan: does the investment make the chicken more consistent, the operation more affordable, or the customer more satisfied? The simulation says yes. The numbers say yes. The chicken that comes out of the automated fryer, held in the automated holding unit, served from the automated service line, is a chicken that tastes like Bojangles—and the operator who serves it is an operator who keeps more of the money that the customer pays. That is the return on investment that matters.
