# Automated Side Preparation and Cooking Machines for Bojangles

## Executive Summary

This document designs an integrated system of machines to automate the preparation and cooking of Bojangles' side items—specifically Dirty Rice, Macaroni & Cheese, Pinto Beans, Green Beans, Mashed Potatoes, and Grits. Rather than a single universal machine, the recommended architecture is a **modular multi-station system** in which each side has a dedicated preparation module, all coordinated by a central controller that manages recipes, timing, and service-line replenishment. The design draws on proven commercial technologies—combi ovens with intelligent cooking suites, automated dispensing systems, and sensor-driven process control—while adapting them to the specific textures, temperatures, and holding requirements of Southern side dishes. The system integrates directly with the service line through underground or back-of-line transfer mechanisms, automated holding units with timed alerts, and a graphical interface that tells employees exactly what to load, when to load it, and when to refresh. The goal is not to eliminate human labor but to redirect it: machines handle the repetitive, the dangerous, and the error-prone, while employees focus on quality verification and customer service.

## Part One: Design Philosophy and System Architecture

### 1.1 Why Multiple Machines, Not One

The temptation in automation design is to pursue a single universal machine that can cook everything. This is the wrong approach for Bojangles' sides, for three reasons. First, the cooking physics of each side are fundamentally different: rice requires precise moisture absorption and gentle heat, macaroni and cheese requires emulsion stability and gentle holding, beans require long slow hydration and collagen breakdown, and grits require constant agitation to prevent lumping. A single machine that attempts to master all of these would be a compromise that masters none. Second, the volume and timing requirements differ: dirty rice might be prepared in large batches twice per shift, while grits must be refreshed every thirty to forty-five minutes. A modular system allows each side to be produced on its own schedule. Third, maintenance and cleaning are simpler when each module is dedicated to a single product: cross-contamination risks are eliminated, cleaning procedures are product-specific, and a failure in one module does not shut down the entire side line.

The recommended architecture is therefore a **three-tier system**: preparation modules (where raw ingredients are combined and cooked), holding and transfer modules (where finished product is maintained at temperature and moved to the service line), and a central coordination controller (which manages recipes, schedules, and alerts). This architecture is consistent with the interstation control systems described in food processing patents, where individual stations have their own controllers but are coordinated by a master system that uses sensor data from downstream stations to adjust upstream operations . The central controller ensures that the dirty rice module does not begin a new batch if the holding unit is still full, and that the grits module begins a refresh cycle when the service line sensor detects that the current batch is approaching its hold limit.

### 1.2 The Automation Circuit: Sensors, Actuators, and Controllers

Every automated cooking module is built around a control loop: sensors measure conditions, the controller compares those measurements to the recipe, and actuators adjust the process to close the gap. The sensors required for Bojangles' sides include **temperature probes** (for cooking vessel, product internal temperature, and holding unit), **weight or level sensors** (for ingredient dispensing and product inventory), **humidity sensors** (for rice and grits, where moisture is critical), and **optical or infrared sensors** (for browning detection in macaroni and cheese and for fill-level detection in holding pans). These sensor types are standard in food process control and are selected for hygiene, robustness, and compatibility with cleaning chemicals .

The actuators include **heating elements** (electric or steam), **pumps and valves** (for liquid ingredient dispensing and water addition), **motors** (for stirring, agitation, and transfer mechanisms), and **servo or stepper motors** (for precise positioning of dispensing heads or transfer arms). The control circuit for a typical module would include a programmable logic controller (PLC) or a comparable embedded controller, relay modules for switching high-current heating elements, motor driver boards for stepper and servo motors, and analog input modules for temperature and weight sensors. This is the same architecture used in the PancakeMaker prototype documented by Aalto University, which used an ESP32 microcontroller, relay modules for heater control, stepper motor drivers for the pump, and thermistors for temperature feedback—all for a total component cost of approximately €160 . A commercial-grade version for Bojangles would be more robust and more expensive, but the fundamental circuit architecture is identical.

### 1.3 Integration with the Service Line: The Transfer and Holding Layer

The automation does not end at the cooking vessel. The finished side must be transferred to the service line, held at the correct temperature, and monitored for freshness. The recommended integration uses **underground or back-of-line transfer conduits**—essentially insulated tubes or conveyor channels that move product from the cooking module to a holding unit positioned beneath or behind the service counter. This is the "underground cold-to-hot chain" concept described in the broader Bojangles blueprint, adapted here for sides that are cooked in batches rather than portioned individually.

The holding units themselves are **smart holding cabinets** with independent temperature zones, humidity control (for rice and grits), and timed alerts. When a batch is loaded into the holding unit, the employee scans a code or selects the product on the touchscreen, and the system begins a hold timer. The touchscreen displays the time remaining, and when the hold window expires, the system locks the dispensing valve or alerts the employee to discard and refresh. This is a direct application of the holding-time management principle that governs Bojangles' chicken and biscuits, extended to the sides that are equally vulnerable to quality degradation.

## Part Two: Module-by-Module Design

### 2.1 The Rice Module (Dirty Rice and Pinto Beans Base)

The rice module is designed for products that require precise liquid absorption and gentle, even heating. It consists of a jacketed kettle with a capacity of approximately 10 to 15 gallons, equipped with a slow-speed agitator that prevents scorching without breaking the rice grains. The module has two ingredient inputs: a dry ingredient dispenser for rice and seasonings, and a liquid input for broth or water. The cooking sequence is controlled by the central controller, which selects the recipe (dirty rice or pinto beans) and executes the corresponding program.

For dirty rice, the module first receives a measured quantity of rice and a pre-mixed seasoning blend from the dry dispenser. The liquid input adds the correct volume of broth, and the agitator begins a slow cycle. A temperature probe monitors the mixture, and when it reaches the target temperature (approximately 200°F for rice cooking), the controller reduces the heat to a simmer and begins the timed cook cycle. A humidity sensor in the headspace monitors moisture loss, and the controller can add small amounts of liquid if the mixture becomes too dry. When the cook cycle is complete, the agitator stops, and the module signals that the product is ready for transfer. The same module can be used for pinto beans, with a different recipe program that includes a longer cook time and a different agitation pattern.

The module's control circuit includes a PLC with analog inputs for the temperature probe and humidity sensor, a relay output for the heating element (or a steam valve if steam is available), a variable frequency drive for the agitator motor, and digital outputs for the ingredient dispensers. The recipe programs are stored in the PLC's memory and can be updated remotely through the central controller. This architecture allows the same hardware to produce multiple products simply by loading a different recipe, which is the principle behind the iCookingSuite in commercial combi ovens: standardized programs that can be executed by any operator with consistent results .

### 2.2 The Macaroni and Cheese Module

The macaroni and cheese module is the most complex of the side modules because it involves two distinct cooking stages (pasta cooking and sauce preparation) that must be combined at the right moment. The recommended design is a **two-vessel system**: a pasta cooker with a perforated basket that can be lifted and drained, and a sauce kettle with a scraped-surface agitator that prevents the cheese sauce from scorching or breaking. The two vessels are connected by a transfer mechanism that moves the cooked pasta from the basket into the sauce kettle when the sauce reaches the correct consistency.

The pasta cooker uses a simple control loop: a temperature sensor and a timer. The water is brought to a boil, the pasta is added from a dispenser, and the cook time is counted down. The sauce kettle is more sophisticated: it uses a temperature sensor, a viscosity sensor (or a torque sensor on the agitator, which infers viscosity from the resistance the agitator encounters), and a pH sensor (to monitor the emulsion stability). When the sauce reaches the target viscosity and temperature, the controller signals that it is ready for the pasta. The transfer mechanism—a motorized basket lift and tilt—deposits the drained pasta into the sauce kettle, and the agitator combines the two components.

The critical control point in this module is the **emulsion stability** of the cheese sauce. If the sauce is heated too high or agitated too vigorously, the emulsion breaks and the sauce becomes greasy and grainy. The controller must therefore limit the temperature to a maximum of approximately 180°F and control the agitator speed within a narrow range. A conductivity sensor can detect the early stages of emulsion breakdown (as the water and fat phases begin to separate, the conductivity changes), and the controller can respond by reducing the heat and increasing the agitation—or, if the breakdown is too advanced, by alerting the operator to discard the batch. This level of sensor-driven control is consistent with the process control principles described in food engineering literature, where multiple sensor inputs are used to infer complex quality attributes that cannot be measured directly .

### 2.3 The Grits Module

Grits are deceptively simple but operationally demanding: they must be smooth, not lumpy, and they must be held at a consistency that is neither stiff nor watery. The grits module is a jacketed kettle with a **high-shear agitator** that prevents lump formation during the initial addition of grits to the liquid. The critical moment in grits production is the incorporation stage: if the grits are added too quickly or without sufficient agitation, they clump and the clumps cannot be broken down by subsequent stirring. The module addresses this with a **controlled dispersion system**: the grits are dispensed through a vibrating chute or a screw feeder that introduces them in a slow, steady stream, while the agitator runs at high speed to disperse them immediately.

The control loop for the grits module includes a temperature sensor, a level sensor, and a torque sensor on the agitator. The torque sensor is particularly important: as the grits cook and absorb water, the viscosity increases, and the torque required to turn the agitator increases proportionally. The controller uses this torque signal to infer the consistency of the grits and to add liquid if the mixture becomes too stiff. This is a more reliable method than a fixed timer, because the consistency of grits depends on the age and grind of the corn, the humidity of the storage environment, and the exact volume of liquid—all variables that a timer cannot account for. The torque-based control ensures that every batch reaches the target consistency regardless of these variables.

### 2.4 The Green Bean and Vegetable Module

Green beans require a different approach because they are a low-volume, high-frequency side that is best cooked in small batches. The recommended module is a **steam-jacketed kettle with a perforated basket** that can be lifted for draining and a simple agitation mechanism (a gentle rocking motion rather than a continuous stir) that ensures even heat without damaging the beans. The control loop is temperature-based: the steam valve is modulated to maintain the water at a simmer (approximately 200°F), and the cook time is set by the recipe. A color sensor (a simple RGB photodiode) can monitor the beans' color and alert the operator if they begin to lose their bright green hue, which indicates overcooking.

The green bean module is designed for **small-batch, on-demand production** rather than bulk cooking. The service line holding unit for green beans has a smaller capacity than the rice or macaroni units, and the module is programmed to produce a fresh batch every thirty to forty-five minutes rather than every two hours. This frequency matches the holding window for green beans, which is shorter than for more robust sides. The module can be loaded with frozen or fresh beans from a hopper, and the cook cycle is short (approximately fifteen to twenty minutes), allowing the operator to run multiple batches during a shift without significant labor.

### 2.5 The Mashed Potato Module

Mashed potatoes are the most texture-sensitive side on the menu. Overmixing produces a gluey, pasty product; undermixing leaves lumps. The potatoes must be cooked until tender, then mashed or riced with minimal mechanical work, and held at a temperature that keeps them warm without drying them out. The recommended module is a **two-stage system**: a steamer basket for cooking the potatoes, and a separate mashing chamber with a paddle agitator that performs a gentle, intermittent mixing motion rather than a continuous high-speed whip.

The control loop for the potato module includes a temperature probe in the steamer, a weight sensor in the mashing chamber (to measure the potato volume and calculate the correct amount of butter and milk), and a torque sensor on the paddle agitator (to detect when the potatoes have reached the desired consistency). The mashing chamber has separate inlets for melted butter and warm milk, which are added in measured quantities based on the weight of the potatoes. The paddle agitator runs at low speed for a short duration—just enough to incorporate the dairy without developing the starch—and then stops. The controller signals completion, and the transfer mechanism moves the mashed potatoes to the holding unit.

The critical design feature of this module is the **gentle handling** of the potatoes. The paddle geometry is designed to fold and press rather than to shear, and the motor is controlled by a variable frequency drive that limits the maximum torque. If the torque exceeds a threshold (indicating that the potatoes are becoming gluey), the controller stops the agitator and alerts the operator. This is a safety net against the most common failure mode in mashed potato production.

## Part Three: Building the System—Components, Assembly, and Integration

### 3.1 The Bill of Materials for a Single Module

A single cooking module (taking the rice module as the representative example) requires the following components: a stainless steel jacketed kettle (10–15 gallon capacity) with a hinged lid and a bottom discharge valve; a slow-speed agitator with a food-grade stainless steel shaft and paddle, driven by a gearmotor with a variable frequency drive; a temperature probe (RTD or thermocouple) with a sanitary fitting; a humidity sensor in the headspace; a dry ingredient dispenser (a hopper with a screw feeder or a vibratory chute); a liquid input valve and flow meter; a PLC or embedded controller with analog and digital I/O; relay modules for the heating element or steam valve; a touchscreen interface (7–10 inch) for operator interaction; and a transfer pump or conveyor for moving finished product to the holding unit. The total component cost for a commercial-grade module is estimated at $15,000 to $25,000, depending on the capacity and the level of automation. This is significantly higher than the €160 prototype cost cited for the PancakeMaker , but the prototype used consumer-grade components and was not designed for continuous commercial operation.

### 3.2 Assembly and Wiring

The assembly process follows a logical sequence: mechanical assembly first (mounting the kettle, installing the agitator, connecting the dispensers and valves), then sensor and actuator installation (mounting the temperature probe, wiring the relays and motor drives), then controller programming and testing. The wiring should follow standard industrial control panel practices: separation of high-voltage and low-voltage circuits, use of terminal blocks for all connections, labeling of every wire, and inclusion of emergency stop circuits that cut power to all actuators when activated.

The control panel should be mounted in a location that is accessible for maintenance but protected from water and steam. The panel should include a main disconnect switch, circuit breakers for each circuit, the PLC and I/O modules, relay modules, motor drives, and a power supply. The touchscreen interface should be mounted on the front of the machine at a height that is comfortable for the operator (approximately 48 to 54 inches from the floor). The wiring between the panel and the machine components should be routed through conduit or cable trays to protect it from heat, moisture, and mechanical damage.

### 3.3 Programming the Controller

The PLC program for a cooking module follows a **state machine** architecture: the module is always in one of a defined set of states (idle, filling, heating, cooking, holding, transferring, cleaning), and transitions between states are triggered by sensor conditions or operator commands. The recipe for each product is stored as a set of parameters (target temperature, cook time, agitation speed, liquid volume, dispense quantities), and the state machine uses these parameters to control the actuators. This architecture is modular and extensible: adding a new product simply requires adding a new recipe parameter set, not rewriting the control logic.

The central coordination controller runs a higher-level program that manages the interactions between modules and the service line. It receives data from each module (current state, time remaining, product temperature), from the holding units (fill level, hold time remaining), and from the POS system (current demand for each side). It uses this data to schedule production: if the dirty rice holding unit is at 30% capacity and the lunch rush is beginning, the controller starts a new batch in the rice module. If the macaroni holding unit is full and the macaroni module is ready to transfer, the controller delays the transfer until the holding unit has capacity. This scheduling logic is the same principle used in commercial combi ovens' production managers, which organize multiple cooking processes and monitor cooking paths to optimize time and energy use .

### 3.4 Integration with the Service Line

The physical integration with the service line depends on the store layout. In new stores, the modules can be located in a back-of-house preparation area, with insulated transfer conduits running beneath the floor or through the wall to holding units positioned behind the service counter. In existing stores, the modules can be located in the back of the kitchen, and the transfer can be performed manually by employees using insulated carts—a lower-capital approach that still captures most of the automation benefits (consistent cooking, reduced labor at the cooking station) while deferring the cost of the transfer infrastructure.

The holding units on the service line are the interface between the automated system and the customer. Each holding unit has a **touchscreen display** that shows the product name, the fill level, the hold time remaining, and the temperature. When the hold time expires, the display turns red and the unit's dispensing valve (if equipped) locks out. The employee must acknowledge the alert and replace the product before the unit will dispense again. This visible accountability ensures that the holding-time discipline is enforced at the point of service, not just in the back of house.

## Part Four: Operational Workflow and Employee Interaction

### 4.1 The Employee's Role in the Automated Side Kitchen

The employee's interaction with the automated side system is designed around three tasks: **loading raw ingredients**, **confirming recipes**, and **responding to alerts**. The loading task is physical but simple: the employee fills the hoppers with rice, pasta, grits, frozen green beans, or diced potatoes, and connects the liquid supply (broth, water, or milk) to the appropriate input. The hoppers are designed for quick changeover, with color-coded lids and labels that match the recipe selection on the touchscreen. The employee does not measure ingredients; the dispensers are calibrated to deliver the exact quantity specified by the recipe.

The confirmation task is cognitive: the employee selects the product to be cooked on the touchscreen, and the system displays the recipe parameters (cook time, temperature, agitation speed) for confirmation. This step prevents the most common error in automated cooking: loading the wrong ingredient into the wrong hopper or selecting the wrong recipe. The confirmation screen also displays the expected completion time, so the employee can coordinate the cooking with the service line's needs.

The alert-response task is where human judgment is required. The system may alert the employee for several reasons: a hopper is empty, a temperature is out of range, a hold timer has expired, or a sensor has detected an anomaly (such as the torque spike that indicates overmixed mashed potatoes). The touchscreen displays the alert with a clear description and a recommended action. For simple alerts (empty hopper, expired hold timer), the employee can resolve the issue directly. For complex alerts (sensor failure, mechanical fault), the employee stops the module and calls for maintenance. The employee does not troubleshoot the internal systems; their responsibility is to contain the problem and communicate it.

### 4.2 Cleaning and Maintenance

Cleaning is the most labor-intensive part of operating any commercial cooking equipment, and the automated side system is designed to minimize it. Each module has a **clean-in-place (CIP) cycle** that can be initiated from the touchscreen at the end of the shift. The CIP cycle fills the kettle with hot water and food-safe detergent, runs the agitator at a cleaning speed, and drains the water through the discharge valve. A rinse cycle follows, and then a sanitizing cycle. The entire process takes approximately twenty to thirty minutes per module and requires no disassembly. This is a simplified version of the automated cleaning systems used in commercial combi ovens, which recognize the degree of soiling and suggest the appropriate cleaning program .

The maintenance schedule for the modules includes daily checks (temperature probe calibration verification, hopper inspection, valve operation), weekly checks (agitator shaft seal inspection, drive belt tension), and monthly checks (full calibration of all sensors, electrical connection inspection). The touchscreen displays a maintenance reminder when a task is due, and the central controller logs all maintenance activities. The goal is to catch small problems before they become large failures, and to keep the modules operating at peak efficiency.

## Conclusion

The automated side preparation and cooking system proposed in this document is not a futuristic fantasy. It is an assembly of proven technologies—jacketed kettles, agitators, dispensers, sensors, PLCs, and touchscreens—arranged in a modular architecture that matches the specific requirements of each Bojangles side. The system reduces labor at the cooking station, improves consistency by eliminating human variability, and enforces holding-time discipline through sensor-driven alerts. It does not eliminate the employee; it redirects the employee from repetitive cooking tasks to quality verification and customer service.

The Main Ingredient Test applies to every design decision: does this make the dirty rice moister, the macaroni creamier, the grits smoother, or the green beans brighter? The rice module's humidity sensor and slow agitator make the rice moister and prevent scorching. The macaroni module's torque-based emulsion monitoring keeps the sauce creamy and prevents breaking. The grits module's torque-based consistency control eliminates lumps. The green bean module's color sensor prevents overcooking and preserves the bright green hue. Each module passes the test. Each module belongs. The automation is not the point; the food is the point. The machines exist to make the food better, more consistent, and more available—and to free the people who work alongside them to do the work that machines cannot do.
