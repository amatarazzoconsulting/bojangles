# Bojangles Dinner Menu Design, Packaging, and Order Fulfillment Strategy

## Executive Summary

This document designs a dinner menu centered on Country Fried Steak with Gravy and Biscuits, paired with two sides, and addresses the operational infrastructure required to execute it profitably. The dinner menu is deliberately built around **larger portions at higher price points** than the breakfast and lunch menu, because dinner customers arrive with different expectations: they are feeding themselves after a long day, often feeding families, and they are comparing Bojangles not to a breakfast biscuit but to a casual dining meat-and-three plate. The Country Fried Steak Dinner therefore uses a larger steak portion, a more generous gravy ladle, two full-sized biscuits rather than one, and two full side portions rather than the smaller sides that accompany a breakfast combo. The higher price reflects the larger portion and the higher food cost, and it positions Bojangles in the dinner market as a value alternative to casual dining rather than a snack stop. The packaging strategy prioritizes microwave compatibility and heat retention through vented, convection-style containers proven to preserve fried food texture during holding. The order fulfillment strategy proposes dedicated, secure pickup infrastructure positioned away from main counter traffic, using either monitored shelving or code-activated heated lockers to protect order integrity and reduce staff interruption.

## Part One: The Dinner Menu Design — Larger Portions, Higher Price Points

### 1.1 The Portion Philosophy: Dinner Is Not Breakfast With a Different Name

The single most important design decision in a dinner menu is the portion size, because portion size is what separates a dinner from a snack. A breakfast biscuit sandwich is designed to be eaten in five minutes with one hand while driving. A dinner plate is designed to be eaten at a table, with a fork, over twenty minutes, and to leave the customer full. If Bojangles serves a dinner plate that is the same size as its lunch combo, the customer will feel cheated—not because the food was bad, but because the portion did not match the occasion. The dinner menu must therefore be built on a **larger portion architecture** from the ground up, and the price must reflect that larger portion rather than pretending that dinner can be priced like lunch.

The Country Fried Steak Dinner is the anchor of this architecture. The breakfast Steak Biscuit uses a steak portion sized to fit inside a biscuit—approximately 2.5 to 3 ounces. The dinner version uses a steak portion of **5 to 6 ounces**, roughly double the breakfast size, because a dinner steak must be the center of the plate rather than a filling inside a sandwich. The gravy is increased from a spoonful to a **4-ounce ladle**, enough to cover the steak and pool on the plate for dipping the biscuits. The biscuits are increased from one to **two full-sized scratch-made biscuits**, because a dinner plate with a single biscuit looks sparse and because the biscuits are the vehicle for the gravy. The sides are increased from the breakfast side size to **full 5- to 6-ounce portions**, served in separate compartments so that the customer can see the volume they are receiving.

### 1.2 The Cost-to-Price Logic: Why Larger Portions Cost More and Should Be Priced Higher

The larger portion is not free, and the menu price must reflect the actual cost of the food rather than absorbing the increase in the hope of driving volume. The dinner steak portion costs approximately **$1.85** at the 5- to 6-ounce size, compared to approximately $0.95 for the breakfast-sized portion. The two full sides cost approximately **$0.65 combined**, compared to approximately $0.30 for the smaller breakfast sides. The two biscuits cost approximately **$0.40**, compared to $0.20 for one. The gravy and packaging add approximately **$0.30**. The total food cost for the dinner plate is therefore approximately **$3.20**, compared to approximately $1.75 for a breakfast steak biscuit combo. At a 30% food cost target—consistent with Bojangles' overall margin structure—the dinner menu price should be approximately **$10.65**. If the brand wants to launch with a promotional price to drive trial, the floor should be **$8.99**, which still delivers a food cost of approximately 35.6%, acceptable for a limited-time promotion but not sustainable as a permanent price.

The pricing logic must be communicated clearly to franchisees, because the temptation will be to price the dinner plate at the lunch combo price and hope that volume makes up for the lower margin. That temptation must be resisted. A dinner plate priced at $7.99 with a $3.20 food cost delivers a food cost of 40%, which is below the brand's margin standard and leaves little room for labor, packaging, and overhead. A dinner plate priced at $10.65 delivers a food cost of 30%, which is consistent with the brand's standard and allows the franchisee to earn a reasonable return. The larger portion justifies the higher price, and the higher price is what makes the larger portion sustainable. The customer who pays $10.65 for a full dinner plate with a 6-ounce steak, two biscuits, and two full sides is not being overcharged; they are paying for a meal that is genuinely larger and more satisfying than a lunch combo.

### 1.3 The Menu Board and the Portion Signal

The menu board must communicate the larger portion visually and verbally, because the customer cannot weigh the steak before ordering. The board should use **size-comparative language**—"Dinner portion," "Two full sides," "Two scratch-made biscuits"—rather than simply listing the item name. The photography should show the plate at a scale that communicates abundance: the steak covering a significant portion of the plate, the gravy pooled generously, the two biscuits visible, the two sides in separate compartments. The board should also include a **comparison anchor**, such as "Twice the steak of our Steak Biscuit," so that the customer who is familiar with the breakfast item understands immediately that the dinner portion is substantially larger. The comparison anchor does the work that a price tag cannot: it tells the customer why the dinner plate costs more, in terms they already understand.

The menu board should also present the default side pairing prominently, with alternatives listed below in smaller type, guiding the customer toward the combination that best represents the brand while preserving choice. The pricing should be structured so that the default pairing is the base price and substitutions are permitted at no additional charge, removing friction from the ordering process. The board should not offer a "small" or "regular" version of the dinner plate, because offering a smaller version undermines the entire premise that dinner is a larger occasion. The dinner plate is the dinner plate. If a customer wants a smaller meal, they can order from the lunch menu, which remains available through the afternoon and evening.

### 1.4 The Recommended Sides and Their Portion Specifications

The two sides accompanying the Country Fried Steak Dinner should be selected to balance the plate's richness and to showcase Bojangles' Southern identity, and they should be served at the full dinner portion size rather than the smaller breakfast side size. The recommended default pairing is **Cajun Pinto Beans (6 ounces) and Dirty Rice (6 ounces)**. The pinto beans provide a savory, slightly spicy counterpoint to the creamy gravy and the fried steak, and the dirty rice adds a textured, seasoned component that reinforces the Cajun flavor profile of the brand. Both sides are hearty enough to stand up to the steak and the gravy, and both are distinct enough from each other that the plate does not feel repetitive. Together, they create a plate that is cohesive, satisfying, and distinctly Bojangles.

Alternative side pairings should be offered for customer choice, all at the 6-ounce dinner portion. **Macaroni and Cheese** paired with **Green Beans** offers a milder, comfort-focused option for customers who prefer less spice. **Mashed Potatoes and Gravy** paired with **Coleslaw** creates a classic meat-and-three profile, though the double gravy (on the steak and on the potatoes) may be excessive for some customers. **Bo Fries** paired with **Coleslaw** offers a lighter option for customers who want the familiarity of fries with the freshness of slaw. Each alternative is priced the same as the default pairing, because the portion size is the same and the food cost differences are minor. The customer chooses based on flavor preference, not on price, which simplifies the ordering decision and reduces the time the customer spends at the counter or in the drive-thru.

### 1.5 The Dinner Daypart Strategy and the Higher-Price Positioning

The Country Fried Steak Dinner should be positioned as a value-oriented, hearty dinner option that competes with casual dining meat-and-three plates at a quick-service price point, and the higher price point is part of that positioning. A casual dining meat-and-three plate typically costs $12 to $16 and requires a sit-down experience with table service and a tip. The Bojangles dinner plate at $10.65 offers a comparable portion at a lower price, with the convenience of counter service or drive-thru pickup. The customer who chooses Bojangles for dinner is not choosing the cheapest option; they are choosing the option that delivers the most food and the most flavor for the price. The higher price point signals that the plate is a full meal rather than a snack, and it attracts customers who are willing to pay for a satisfying dinner rather than settling for a smaller, cheaper item.

The daypart strategy should recognize that dinner is Bojangles' weakest occasion relative to breakfast and lunch, and the higher price point must be supported by marketing that justifies it. The Country Fried Steak Dinner should be launched as a limited-time offering with a promotional price of **$8.99** for the first ninety days to drive trial, with the regular price of **$10.65** taking effect after the promotional period. The marketing should emphasize the plate's completeness and its larger portion—"a full dinner, not just a chicken combo"—and it should use the comparison anchor to explain the value. If the item achieves target sales and margin thresholds during the promotional period, it should be made a permanent menu addition at the regular price. The item should be available from 4:00 p.m. until close, with the option to extend availability if customer demand warrants.

## Part Two: To-Go Packaging for Microwave Compatibility and Heat Retention

### 2.1 The Packaging Challenge: Fried Food and Moisture Management

The central packaging challenge for the Country Fried Steak Dinner is moisture, and the challenge is greater for the dinner plate than for the breakfast biscuit because the dinner plate has more components and a larger volume of fried food. Fried foods release steam as they cool, and in a sealed container, that steam condenses on the lid and drips back onto the food, turning crispy breading into soggy breading. The 6-ounce steak is particularly vulnerable because its breaded surface has a high surface area relative to its mass, and the two biscuits are similarly vulnerable because a biscuit that steams in a closed container becomes dense and gummy within minutes. The gravy, by contrast, benefits from retained moisture and should be packaged separately to prevent it from migrating into the steak and biscuits before the customer is ready to eat. The larger portion size means more steam, more condensation, and a greater need for ventilation than a breakfast order requires.

The packaging solution must therefore accomplish three things simultaneously: allow steam to escape, maintain internal temperature, and keep the components separated until assembly by the customer. A single-compartment clamshell cannot accomplish all three, and a small clamshell cannot accommodate the larger dinner portion at all. The recommended approach is a **multi-component system sized for the dinner portion**: a large vented container for the steak and two biscuits, a separate sealed 4-ounce container for the gravy, and separate 6-ounce containers or compartments for the two sides. This system requires more packaging material than a breakfast order, but it preserves the integrity of each component and gives the customer control over assembly. The cost of the packaging is included in the $3.20 food cost estimate and is reflected in the $10.65 menu price, which is why the dinner plate cannot be priced at the breakfast price.

### 2.2 The Recommended Container: Vented Convection Packaging Sized for Dinner

The steak and biscuits should be packaged in a container designed specifically for fried foods and sized for the dinner portion. Anchor Packaging's Crisp Food Technologies containers use a convection cross-flow design with through-the-closure ventilation and raised airflow channels in the base to relieve moisture and condensation while maintaining product temperature. Independent testing showed that these containers preserved the temperature and texture of fried foods longer than competitive materials during a thirty-minute delivery window. The containers are microwave-safe, withstand temperatures up to 230°F under heat lamps and in warming units, and are made from polypropylene with a renewable mineral additive. For Bojangles, this means the dinner steak and two biscuits can be held in a warming unit before pickup, reheated by the customer if necessary, and still deliver an acceptable texture.

The container must be sized for the dinner portion, not the breakfast portion. A 5- to 6-ounce steak plus two biscuits requires a container with a footprint of approximately 9 by 6 inches and a depth of at least 3 inches, so that the biscuits are not crushed and the steak has room to breathe. The container should have a vented lid that allows steam to escape but does not allow the food to spill if the container is tilted. The gravy should be packaged in a separate sealed 4-ounce cup with a secure lid, filled to approximately 80% capacity to allow for expansion during heating and to prevent leakage. The sides should be packaged in separate 6-ounce containers or in a compartmented container that prevents migration of flavors and moisture. The entire system should be assembled in a branded outer bag or carrier that keeps the components together and allows the customer to transport the meal without spilling. The bag should be sized for the dinner portion, with a flat bottom that allows it to sit securely on a car seat or a counter.

### 2.3 Microwave Reheating Instructions and Customer Communication

The packaging should include clear, simple reheating instructions printed on the lid or on an insert card, and the instructions should be calibrated for the larger dinner portion rather than the breakfast portion. The instructions should specify the microwave power level (medium-high, approximately 70%), the recommended time (90 to 120 seconds for the steak and two biscuits, 45 to 60 seconds for the gravy, 60 to 90 seconds for the two sides), and the importance of removing the gravy lid before heating. The instructions should also note that the steak and biscuits should be heated in the vented container without additional covering, allowing the steam to escape and the breading to remain crispy. These instructions are not a guarantee of restaurant-quality reheating, but they give the customer the best chance of a satisfactory experience and demonstrate that Bojangles has considered the customer's needs beyond the point of sale.

The packaging should also communicate the brand's commitment to quality and the value of the larger portion. A simple message on the container—"A full dinner, packed hot. Reheat to restore the crunch."—acknowledges the reality of takeout food while framing the reheating process as a continuation of the Bojangles experience rather than an admission of compromise. The message should also remind the customer that the plate includes two biscuits and two full sides, reinforcing the value proposition at the moment of unboxing. The customer who feels that the brand has anticipated their needs and delivered a complete meal is more likely to order takeout again, even if the reheated product is not identical to the dine-in experience.

## Part Three: Order-Ahead Placement, Organization, and Secure Pickup Infrastructure

### 3.1 The Current State: Order-Ahead Exists but Fulfillment Is Inconsistent

Bojangles already offers order-ahead functionality through its app and website, with curbside pickup and delivery available at many locations. However, customer reviews reveal significant friction in the fulfillment process. One customer reported placing a large order through the app with ten minutes' notice, then waiting over thirty minutes at the restaurant and being unable to check in at the curbside spot because the app would not recognize their arrival. Another review described waiting forty-five minutes in a designated pickup spot before going inside to discover the order had not been prepared as expected. These are not isolated complaints; they reflect a systemic gap between the promise of order-ahead convenience and the reality of in-store execution. The gap is particularly damaging for a dinner order, because a dinner customer who is waiting forty-five minutes with a family in the car is a customer who will not order dinner from Bojangles again.

The root cause is not the app itself but the absence of dedicated fulfillment infrastructure. When order-ahead customers arrive at the restaurant, their orders are competing for space and staff attention with walk-in customers, drive-thru traffic, and delivery driver pickups. The orders may be sitting on the main counter, where they are vulnerable to being mixed up, knocked over, or taken by the wrong person. The staff member who prepared the order may be the same person who is trying to take a walk-in order, answer the phone, and monitor the drive-thru. The order-ahead customer becomes an interruption rather than a priority, and the experience suffers accordingly. The larger dinner portion makes the problem worse, because a dinner order takes up more counter space and is more visible to walk-in customers who may mistake it for an unclaimed order.

### 3.2 The Solution: Dedicated Pickup Zone with Secure Shelving

The recommended solution is a dedicated pickup zone positioned away from the main counter and drive-thru traffic, equipped with shelving or cubbies that are monitored by staff but not dependent on staff presence for every transaction. Restaurant design guidance recommends placing pickup areas near the expo station—the point where orders are finished and bagged—so that the staff member responsible for order accuracy and completeness can monitor the pickup zone without leaving their post. The zone should be visually distinct, with signage, lighting, or flooring that directs order-ahead customers to the correct location and signals to walk-in customers that the area is reserved for pickup. The zone should be sized for the dinner portion, with shelves deep enough to hold the large vented container and the separate side and gravy containers without crowding.

The shelving itself should be configured for order separation and visibility. Each order should have a designated slot, labeled with the customer's name or order number, and the slot should be sized to accommodate the full dinner meal (steak container, gravy cup, two side containers, and two biscuits). The shelving should be open or semi-open so that staff can see which orders are waiting and customers can see that their order is ready without needing to ask. This visibility reduces the anxiety of waiting and allows the customer to self-verify that their order is present before approaching the counter. The shelving should be positioned so that it is not directly accessible from the public sidewalk or parking lot, reducing the risk of theft, while remaining easily reachable by customers who have entered the store. The zone should also include a small counter or table where customers can assemble their meal—pouring the gravy, opening the side containers—without blocking the pickup shelves for other customers.

### 3.3 The Advanced Option: Heated Lockers with Code-Activated Access

For higher-volume locations or stores where theft or order mix-ups are a persistent concern, heated lockers offer a more secure and more temperature-controlled solution. Hatco's Flav-R 2-Go locker system provides thermostatically controlled heated compartments with a digital touchscreen interface, separate heat and timer controls for each shelf, and a heated air curtain that maintains consistent temperatures. Orders are placed in the locker by staff, and the customer receives a code that unlocks the specific compartment. The locker keeps the order hot and secure until the customer arrives, eliminating the window during which an order sits on a shelf and is vulnerable to being taken or going cold. The lockers are available in configurations with multiple compartment sizes, so the larger dinner container can be accommodated alongside the standard breakfast and lunch orders.

The locker system also solves the staffing problem. Staff do not need to monitor the pickup zone continuously because the lockers are self-service once the order is loaded. The system can be integrated with the POS so that when an order is marked complete, the corresponding locker opens automatically or a code is generated and sent to the customer. Carter-Hoffmann's PUC cabinets offer similar functionality with heated, ambient, and combination configurations, and the company emphasizes that lockers reduce labor costs, lower food waste, and improve food safety by keeping orders protected and organized. The capital investment is significant—lockers are more expensive than shelving—but the operational savings and the improvement in customer experience may justify the cost in high-volume locations. For a dinner program that is trying to build a new daypart, the reliability of the pickup experience is worth the investment, because a single bad dinner pickup experience can lose a customer for the entire daypart.

### 3.4 Operational Workflow: From Order Placement to Customer Pickup

The operational workflow for order-ahead fulfillment should be standardized across all stores. When an order is placed through the app or website, it enters the kitchen display system with a timestamp and an estimated ready time. The kitchen prepares the order according to the same standards as a walk-in order, but with the awareness that the order will be held for pickup rather than consumed immediately. The dinner order requires additional coordination because it has more components—steak, gravy, two sides, two biscuits—and each component must be packaged correctly and placed in the pickup slot or locker together. A dinner order that is missing the gravy or the second biscuit is a dinner order that will generate a complaint and a refund request, so the pickup coordinator must verify the completeness of each dinner order before it is placed in the pickup zone.

When the order is complete, the staff member bags it in the designated packaging, labels it with the customer's name and order number, and places it in the assigned pickup slot or locker. The system sends an automatic notification to the customer that the order is ready, with the pickup location and any instructions for retrieving the order. When the customer arrives, they proceed to the pickup zone, locate their order by name or number (or unlock the locker with their code), and take it without needing to interact with the counter staff. If the customer needs assistance—for example, if the order is not present or if they have a question—a staff member should be available nearby but not stationed directly at the pickup zone. This model reduces the counter congestion that occurs when order-ahead customers and walk-in customers compete for the same staff attention, and it gives the order-ahead customer a sense of control and efficiency that is the entire point of ordering ahead.

### 3.5 Staffing and Training Implications

The pickup zone model requires a shift in staffing priorities. One employee per shift should be designated as the **pickup coordinator**, responsible for monitoring the pickup zone, loading lockers or shelving, verifying the completeness of dinner orders, and responding to customer issues. This role can be combined with the expo or drive-thru coordinator role in lower-volume stores, but in higher-volume stores it should be a dedicated position. The pickup coordinator should be trained on the locker or shelving system, the order-ahead workflow, the dinner packaging standards, and the customer service protocols for handling missing or incorrect orders. The training should emphasize that the pickup customer is not an interruption; they are a customer who has already committed to the brand and whose experience at the pickup zone determines whether they will order ahead again.

The kitchen staff should also be trained on the packaging standards for order-ahead dinner meals. The steak and two biscuits must be placed in the large vented container while still hot, the gravy must be sealed separately in a 4-ounce cup, and the two sides must be portioned at 6 ounces each and packaged according to the standard. A meal that is assembled incorrectly—gravy on the steak instead of on the side, sides mixed together, only one biscuit instead of two—is a meal that will generate a complaint and a refund request. The packaging standard is not a detail; it is the product. The customer who receives a correctly packaged dinner meal is a customer who will trust the order-ahead system again, and the customer who trusts the system is the customer who will pay the higher dinner price without hesitation.

## Conclusion

The Country Fried Steak Dinner is a logical extension of Bojangles' existing product line, requiring no new equipment and minimal new training. Its success depends not on the novelty of the menu item but on three things: the **larger portion** that justifies the higher price, the **packaging** that preserves the food's quality during transport, and the **fulfillment infrastructure** that ensures the customer receives what they ordered, when they expected it. The larger portion is the foundation: a 6-ounce steak, two biscuits, and two 6-ounce sides at $10.65 is a fair price for a full dinner, and the customer who receives it will feel that they got what they paid for. The vented convection packaging and the dedicated pickup zone are not glamorous investments, but they are the operational infrastructure on which the dinner menu's profitability rests. A steak dinner that arrives soggy and cold, or that is missing from the pickup shelf when the customer arrives, is a steak dinner that will not be ordered again—no matter how large the portion or how reasonable the price.

The final consideration is the Main Ingredient Test: does this make the steak hotter, the biscuits fresher, the pickup faster, or the customer more confident? The larger portion makes the dinner more satisfying. The vented container makes the steak hotter and the biscuits fresher by allowing moisture to escape. The dedicated pickup zone makes the pickup faster by removing the order-ahead customer from the counter queue. The secure shelving or lockers make the customer more confident that their order will be there when they arrive. Each element passes the test. Each element belongs.
